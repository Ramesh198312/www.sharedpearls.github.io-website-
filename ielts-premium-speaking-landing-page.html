History

The test format

Three parts, eleven to fourteen minutes

The Speaking test is a face-to-face conversation with one examiner, recorded from start to finish. The same format is used for Academic and General Training.

  1. 1

    Part 1 · Introduction and interview 4–5 minutes

    The examiner checks your name, then asks about familiar topics such as your home, work, studies and interests, usually three topics with three or four questions each. Answers of two to four sentences are ideal.

    In these tests: a recording after every question.

  2. 2

    Part 2 · Individual long turn 3–4 minutes

    You receive a task card with a topic and points to cover, plus paper and a pencil. You have one minute to prepare, then speak for one to two minutes without interruption. The examiner may finish with one or two short questions.

    In these tests: a notes pad, a one-minute timer, then one long recording.

  3. 3

    Part 3 · Two-way discussion 4–5 minutes

    The examiner asks broader, more abstract questions linked to the Part 2 topic. This is where you show you can explain, compare, speculate and justify opinions about society, not just your own life.

    In these tests: a recording after every follow-up question.

How speaking is assessed

Four criteria, equally weighted

Your band is the average of four scores, each from 0 to 9. Here is what each one means in plain words, and what moves it up.

FC

Fluency and coherence

How smoothly you keep talking and how easy you are to follow: speaking at length without long hesitations, and linking ideas in a logical order.

Raise it: give a reason or example after every answer; use linkers such as what’s more, that said, as a result; pause for ideas, not for words.

LR

Lexical resource

The range and precision of your vocabulary: using less common words and natural collocations, and finding another way to say something when a word is missing.

Raise it: learn phrases, not single words; use topic vocabulary precisely; paraphrase the question instead of repeating it.

GRA

Grammatical range and accuracy

How many structures you use and how accurately: mixing simple and complex sentences, and keeping errors from getting in the way of meaning.

Raise it: use conditionals to speculate, relative clauses to add detail and a range of tenses to tell a story; fix repeated slips.

P

Pronunciation

How easily you are understood: clear individual sounds, natural word and sentence stress, rhythm and intonation that support your meaning.

Raise it: stress the key word in each idea; group words into short chunks; listen back to your recordings and copy the sample-answer rhythm.

Band 5Keeps going on familiar topics but relies on repetition, simple language and frequent self-correction.
Band 6Willing to speak at length; meaning is clear, though errors and some loss of coherence appear.
Band 7Speaks at length with ease; flexible vocabulary and complex structures, with only occasional errors.
Band 8+Fluent and precise; idiomatic language and complex grammar used naturally, with rare slips.

Strategy guides

What strong candidates do in each part

Part 1

Answer, extend, stop

  • Answer the question directly in your first sentence.
  • Extend with one reason, example or contrast.
  • Stop after two to four sentences; the examiner has more questions to ask.
  • Show range early: avoid one-word answers and avoid memorised speeches.
  • If you don’t do something, say so and talk about why.

Part 2

Plan the story, not the sentences

  • In the minute, write key words for each point, not full sentences.
  • Open with a clear one-line introduction of your choice.
  • Cover the points in order, adding a small story or detail to each.
  • Save time for the final “explain” point; it is where opinions shine.
  • Keep going until the examiner stops you: aim for the full two minutes.

Part 3

Think like a commentator

  • Move from your own life to people and society in general.
  • Give a view, a reason, an example and a balancing point.
  • Speculate about the future: it’s likely that…, I suspect…
  • Compare groups, places or periods: young and old, now and then.
  • It’s fine to say a question is difficult, as long as you then explore it.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a microphone?

Yes, to record yourself. Any built-in laptop or phone microphone works. Your browser will ask for permission the first time. Without a microphone you can still follow each test with timed answer spaces.

Where are my recordings stored?

Only on this device, in your browser’s storage. Nothing is uploaded. You can download any answer from the review screen, and clearing your browser data or pressing “Reset everything” removes them.

Should I choose Exam or Practice mode?

Use Practice mode to learn a test’s topics, replay questions and compare your answers with the samples. Use Exam mode for a realistic run-through: the examiner moves on automatically, just as in the real test.

How accurate is my band?

It is as accurate as your self-rating. For a fairer picture, listen to your recordings the next day, compare them with the sample answers and use the band descriptions on the review screen. A teacher or study partner can rate them too.

Should I memorise the sample answers?

No. Examiners notice rehearsed answers and they do not reflect your real level. Borrow structures and phrases, then answer with your own ideas and experiences.

What if I don’t know anything about a topic?

Say so briefly, then talk about what you do know, or imagine a likely situation. You are assessed on your language, not your knowledge.

Can I use these tests for Academic and General Training?

Yes. The Speaking test is the same for both.

Your voice stays with you

Recordings and progress are saved only in this browser. Live transcripts, if you switch them on, use your browser’s own speech recognition service. Export your progress to move it to another device.
