Hedging & Boosting

Hedging and boosting are rhetorical strategies that regulate the strength, certainty, commitment, and interpersonal force of academic claims.

  • Hedges reduce the strength or categorical force of a proposition.
  • Boosters increase the writer's expressed certainty or rhetorical force.
  • Both are central resources of academic stance, not symptoms of confidence or doubt.
  • Effective academic writing calibrates claim strength rather than asserting uniform certainty.
  • Appropriate strength depends on evidence, discipline, genre, audience, and communicative purpose.

A note on terms: hedging is not simply “being uncertain,” and boosting is not simply “being confident.” Both are rhetorical resources for managing authorial commitment and reader interpretation — independent of how confident the writer actually feels.

Two instruments, one purpose: calibrated commitment

Neither device is inherently preferable. Skilled academic writers move between hedging and boosting sentence by sentence, matching expressed commitment to what the evidence will actually bear.

H

Hedging

Hedges soften the categorical force of a claim. They mark a proposition as probable, possible, partial, or contingent rather than established fact — signalling that the writer is aware of the limits of the evidence, alternative explanations, or the boundaries of a claim's scope.

Hedges operate through modal verbs, epistemic adverbs and adjectives, epistemic nouns, tentative reporting verbs, probability expressions, approximators, vague quantifiers, impersonal and conditional constructions.

Boosting

Boosters increase the expressed certainty or force of a claim. They present a proposition as established, self-evident, or beyond reasonable dispute — projecting authority, consensus, and the weight of strong or converging evidence.

Boosters operate through strong modal expressions, emphatic adverbs, certainty adjectives and nouns, strong reporting verbs, intensifiers, and categorical constructions.

“

Neither device is a report of the writer's inner state. A hedge does not mean the writer privately doubts the claim, and a booster does not mean the writer feels certain. Both are rhetorical resources for managing authorial commitment — the strength with which a proposition is presented to a reader — and for shaping how that reader is invited to interpret and respond to the claim. Boosters, in particular, should be used responsibly: their force should be proportionate to the evidence that supports them, not to the writer's enthusiasm for the finding.

SEMANTIC & PRAGMATIC DEPTH

Where syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and rhetoric meet

Hedging and boosting are not decorative word choices. They sit at the intersection of several linguistic levels at once — and the same proposition produces different effects at each level depending on how strongly it is expressed.

Epistemic meaning

How writers communicate certainty, probability, possibility, inference, and evidential commitment — the relationship between what is claimed and how confidently it is claimed to be known.

Interpersonal meaning

How stance markers anticipate reader resistance, reduce face threat toward readers who may disagree, signal scholarly caution, establish authority, and position the writer within a disciplinary community.

Pragmatic meaning: one proposition, three effects

The same underlying claim — that an intervention affected an outcome — produces markedly different reader expectations depending on its expressed force.

DISCIPLINARY CONTEXT

How strong should your claim be?

Appropriate hedging and boosting are not fixed rules applied uniformly across academic writing. They vary with disciplinary convention, the type of evidence a field values, its genre expectations, and its epistemological tradition. No discipline is simply “more hedged” or “more boosted” than another — each calibrates stance to what counts as warrant within it.
