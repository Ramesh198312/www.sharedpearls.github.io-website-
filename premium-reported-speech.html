01 — Filing the Report

What reported speech does

Reported speech is when you tell somebody else what you, or another person, said before. The quotation marks disappear, the capital letter that once opened someone's exact words is gone, and a reporting verb — say, said, say that, said that — steps in to carry the message instead.

The word that is the usual thread connecting the reporting verb to the reported words, though everyday speech often drops it. Keep it only where its absence would blur the meaning.

The students said (that) they wanted to go.
The students told {us / John / the teacher} (that) they wanted to go.

Notice the difference between say and tell: tell always needs a listener named directly after it (told us, told John), while say stands alone unless followed by to.

Direct speech  →  reported speech
Direct speechReported speech
I like fish.She says that she likes fish.
I'm visiting Paris next weekend.She said that she was visiting Paris the following weekend.
02 — One Step Backward

The tense moves back in time

The most reliable guideline in reported speech: the tense moves one step backward in time — but never further back than the past perfect. A present becomes a past; a past becomes a past perfect; a past perfect stays exactly where it is, because there is nowhere further back to go.

The exception Despite this pleasing regularity, it isn't always true. If a situation is still true in the present, the reporter may choose a present form or the "one step backward" form.

Direct: "I live in Paris."  →  Reported: She said she lives in Paris.  or  She said she lived in Paris.
In everyday speech

The colloquial shortcut

Careful writing keeps the past perfect wherever the rule calls for it. Casual speech often doesn't — it substitutes an ordinary past tense unless the exact sequence of events genuinely needs clarifying.

Direct speech"Correct" reported speechColloquial alternative
I went to London.He said he had been to London.He said he went to London.
I've bought the tickets.She said she had bought the tickets.She said she bought the tickets.
I was laughing all evening.She said she'd been laughing all evening.She said she was laughing all evening.
03 — Who, When, Where

Pronouns, time and place shift too

A report is filed from a different vantage point than the original words — a different speaker, often a different moment and place. Three kinds of detail move to match that new vantage point: the pronouns, the time expressions, and the place words.

1st

First person

Pronouns such as I / my / me / we are changed according to the subject of the reporting verb.

"I like fish." → She said she liked fish.
2nd

Second person

The pronoun you / your is changed according to the object of the reporting verb.

"You are late," she told him. → She told him he was late.
3rd

Third person

Pronouns such as he / she / they are usually not changed at all.

"They left early," he said. → He said they had left early.

These only shift when the context has genuinely changed. A conversation reported in the very room where it happened will still sound natural with "here" — the shift follows the real distance between the two tellings, not a mechanical rule.

04 — Filing a Question

Reported questions keep their shape, lose their punch

A question stays a question in meaning, but it stops behaving like one grammatically: the question mark disappears, and — most importantly — the subject and verb are never inverted back to statement order.

With a question word (what, why, where, how…)
Direct speechReported speech
Why don't you speak English?He asked me why I didn't speak English.
How do you know about it?She asked me how I knew about it.
Without a question word (yes / no questions)
Direct speechReported speech
Do you speak English?He asked me whether / if I spoke English.
Can I extend my holiday?I asked if I could extend my holiday.
A common slip
Include the wh- word, but never invert the subject and the verb back to question order.
He asked me why was I late.
He asked me why I was late.
05 — Requests, Commands & Exclamations

Every sentence type has its own connective

Statements, questions, commands, wishes and exclamations don't all report the same way. Each carries its own connecting word into the reported form.

Sentence typeConnectiveExample
Assertive that I want to study hard.He tells me he wants to study hard.
Interrogative wh- word / if / whether Where do you live?She asked me where I lived.
Imperative to  /  not to Come back tomorrow.The director told us to come back the following day.
Do not eat junk food.The doctor advised me not to eat junk food.
Optative that May God bless you.My mother wished me that God might bless me.
Exclamatory that Wow, Nepal is a beautiful country!The tourist exclaimed with surprise that Nepal was a beautiful country.
06 — Beyond Said and Told

A wider vocabulary of reporting

Said and asked are the two workhorse verbs — but a precise reporting verb can carry tone and intention that said never could. Demand, for instance, means to ask in a forceful way.

07 — Fifteen Dispatches

Practice: file the report correctly

Choose the reported version that a careful correspondent would file for each of the following. Select an answer for every dispatch, then check your work.

0 / 15
01
They said, "She was selling her soul to the devil."
Past continuous moves one step further back to past perfect continuous — the ongoing action is framed as already underway at the time reported.
02
I said, "It is raining cats and dogs."
Present continuous steps back one place to past continuous — "is raining" becomes "was raining."
03
The Army General said, "The troop won the war ultimately."
Past simple moves one step further back to past perfect: "won" becomes "had won."
04
Sammy said to me, "He has a habit of chopping and changing his decisions at the eleventh hour."
"Said to me" becomes "told me"; present simple "has" moves back one step to "had."
05
She said, "God is great."
A permanent truth is one of the moments the backward shift can be set aside — the present tense is kept exactly as it was said.
06
Kanika said, "Could the parents complain against the teachers?"
A yes/no question is reported with "whether" or "if" and takes the reporting verb "asked," not "said." "Could" was already backshifted, so it stays.
07
Jolly said to Miss Bill, "How will you solve it as there is no time?"
The subject and verb are never inverted back to question order — "how she would," not "how would she." "Said to" becomes "asked" for a question.
08
She said, "Where do they spend their holidays?"
No auxiliary "did" survives into the reported clause, and present simple "spend" shifts back to "spent."
09
Sanjay said, "When did they vote against that corrupt minister?"
The underlying tense here is past simple ("did … vote"), which moves one step further back to past perfect: "had voted."
10
They said, "Let us continue our hard work with zeal and honesty."
A "let us" proposal is a suggestion, not a command aimed at a listener — suggested that … should captures it precisely.
11
Tom said to Mary, "I will help you with your homework."
First person "I" follows the subject (Tom → he); second person "you" follows the object (Mary → her). Also, "say" is never followed directly by a person's name without "to."
12
On Monday she said, "I'll finish the report tomorrow."
"Tomorrow," spoken on Monday, is filed later as "the next day" — the time expression moves with the shift in vantage point, and "will" becomes "would."
13
The doctor said, "Do not skip your medication."
A negative imperative is reported with "not to" after a suitable reporting verb — here, "advised … not to skip."
14
"Bravo! What a magnificent performance!" the critic said.
An exclamation of praise or surprise is best carried by "exclaimed," which preserves the emotional charge that "mentioned" would flatten.
15
Speaking in London, he said, "I must be here by nine tomorrow."
"Must" of obligation becomes "had to"; "here" and "tomorrow" shift to "there" and "the next day" once the report is filed from another time and place.
