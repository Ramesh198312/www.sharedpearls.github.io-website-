The Study
of Sounds.

Before English can be spelled, it has to be spoken — and speech begins in a narrow tract of muscle, cartilage and bone that turns moving air into meaning. This is the map of that tract, and the sounds it makes.

Opens, as generations of students' textbooks have, with T. S. Watt's 1954 verse on the strange distance between English spelling and English sound.

02 · Phonetics

Slowing down the chatter of everyday talk

As we speak, we don't produce a random selection of the sounds our vocal tract can make — only certain sounds are selected, on a regular basis, as meaningful. Identifying and describing them means slowing that stream down to look at each individual segment. Symbols for these sounds, drawn from the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), are always written inside square brackets — [p], [ʃ], [aɪ].

Articulatory

How sounds are made

The study of how speech sounds are articulated — which parts of the vocal tract move, and how. This is the branch this page focuses on.

Acoustic

How sounds travel

Deals with the physical properties of speech as sound waves moving through the air.

03 · Consonants

Three features describe every consonant

When we describe how a consonant is articulated, we focus on three things: whether it is voiced or voiceless, where in the vocal tract it is made (place of articulation), and how the airflow is shaped there (manner of articulation).

Voiceless

Vocal folds spread apart

Air from the lungs passes between the vocal folds with no obstruction, producing a voiceless sound — no vibration.

Voiced

Vocal folds drawn together

Air repeatedly pushes the folds apart as it passes through, vibrating them, producing a voiced sound.

Try it yourself Place a fingertip gently on your Adam's apple and say Z-Z-Z-Z or V-V-V-V — you should feel a vibration, since these are voiced. Now try S-S-S-S or F-F-F-F: no vibration, since these are voiceless. The same contrast can be felt with a finger in each ear.

Place of articulation

Once air passes the larynx, it travels up through the pharynx and out through the mouth and/or nose. Most consonants are formed by using the tongue and other parts of the mouth to constrict the shape of this passage at a particular place.

Sounds, not letters Spelling is a poor guide to pronunciation. Bang and tongue end in [ŋ] alone — there is no [g] despite the spelling. Ship is a single sound [ʃ], not [s]+[h]. Photo and enough are both pronounced with [f]. The first and last letters of write, and the middle two of right, are silent — both are [raɪt]. And listen closely: face ends in [s], but phase ends in [z].

Manner of articulation

[t] and [s] are both voiceless alveolars, yet clearly different sounds — the difference lies in how the airflow is shaped as it passes: their manner of articulation.

Stops

Block, then release

Airflow is briefly blocked, then let go abruptly — almost a small explosion. [p t k b d g]

Fricatives

Squeeze through a gap

Airflow is almost blocked, then escapes through a narrow gap, creating friction. [f θ s ʃ h v ð z ʒ]

Affricates

Stop, then fricative

A brief stop combined immediately with a fricative. [tʃ] cheap · [dʒ] jeep

Nasals

Out through the nose

The velum lowers, letting air flow out through the nose. [m n ŋ]

Liquids

Round the sides

The tongue raises and curls, letting airflow escape around its sides. [l r]

Glides

Toward or from a vowel

Also called approximants or semi-vowels — the tongue moves toward or away from a vowel position. [w j]

Glottal stops & flaps A glottal stop [ʔ] closes the glottis completely, then releases it — heard in the middle of "uh-uh," or when Harry Potter or bottle lose their "tt" sound in casual speech. A flap [ɾ] is made by tapping the tongue tip briefly on the alveolar ridge — many American speakers flap [t] and [d] between vowels, so that latter/ladder, metal/medal, and writer/rider sound almost identical.
04 · Vowels

Free-flowing, and always voiced

Where consonants obstruct the airflow, vowels are produced with a relatively free flow of air, and are typically all voiced. We describe them by where the tongue sits: front or back, high or low. Say heat, hit, hat, hot in front of a mirror — your mouth stays fairly closed for the first two, high-front vowels, then opens wider as the tongue lowers for the last two.

Diphthongs: two vowels, one glide

A diphthong is a single sound made of the vocal organs gliding from one vowel position to another. [aɪ] glides low-to-high-front, as in Hi or Bye. [aʊ] glides low-to-high-back, as in the classic training phrase "how now brown cow."

Southern British English typically omits the final [r] that American varieties pronounce — especially among higher social-status speakers.

Subtle individual variation Some speakers make no distinction between the vowels in caught and cot, using [ɑ] for both. The unstressed schwa [ə] — heard in afford, collapse, wanted, and the everyday words a and the — is the most common vowel sound in casual English speech. And the uvula, dangling at the end of the velum, produces uvular sounds such as the French [ʁ] in rouge and lettre.
