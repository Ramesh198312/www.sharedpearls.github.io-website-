Parts of Speech

The Architecture of English

Words do not merely belong to grammatical categories; they perform functions, establish relationships and shape meaning within larger structures.

01 Overview

Beyond labelling

Identifying a word's class is only the first step. Real control of English grammar comes from asking what a word is doing in a given sentence — not simply what it is called.

The classic eight parts of speech — noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction and interjection — are grammatical categories that classify words according to their form, syntactic behaviour, function and meaning. This lesson treats them as components of a larger architecture: words combine into phrases, phrases into clauses, clauses into sentences, and only at that point does full meaning emerge.

An analytical framework

Throughout this lesson, apply the same six questions to any word you are analysing.

1

What word class is it?

2

What form does it have?

3

What function does it perform?

4

What does it modify or relate to?

5

What meaning does it contribute?

6

What would change with a different form?

A single lexical item can belong to different word classes depending on how it is used: I work every day. (verb) versus The work was demanding. (noun). The form is identical; only context reveals the class.

02 Word-Class Architecture

What is a part of speech?

A part of speech is a grammatical category used to classify words according to their form, their syntactic behaviour, the function they perform, and the kind of meaning they typically contribute.

Content words and function words

Content words

Carry the main descriptive meaning of a sentence: nouns, lexical verbs, adjectives and many adverbs. This is an open class — new members are added constantly.

Function words

Signal grammatical relationships rather than describing the world directly: pronouns, determiners, auxiliary verbs, prepositions and conjunctions. This is a comparatively closed, stable class.

Classification systems differ slightly between grammars; where terminology varies (for instance, whether determiners are treated as a distinct class or as a type of pronoun/adjective), this lesson uses the most widely taught convention and flags the distinction explicitly rather than silently picking a side.

Word class versus grammatical function

The same word class can perform different functions, and the same function can be filled by different word classes. This distinction is one of the central intellectual tools in this lesson.

Word classPossible functions
Nounsubject · object · complement
Verbpredicator (head of the verb phrase)
Adjectivemodifier · subject complement
Adverbmodifier of a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause
Prepositionhead of a prepositional phrase

Form versus function

Do not confuse a word's form (its shape, e.g. an -ing ending) with the function it performs in a particular sentence.

Swimming is enjoyable.

The -ing form functions here as a noun-like subject (a gerund).

I saw him swimming.

The identical -ing form now functions within a non-finite verb phrase, describing an ongoing action.

03 Nouns

Nouns

Nouns name entities, but their grammar is defined less by meaning than by how they behave: what determiners they take, whether they pluralise, and what functions they perform in a clause.

Common / Proper

city vs Tokyo — proper nouns name a unique entity and are capitalised.

Concrete / Abstract

table vs honesty — abstract nouns often derive from adjectives or verbs.

Count / Non-count

idea/ideas vs information — non-count nouns resist pluralisation and the indefinite article.

Collective

committee, team — singular in form, denoting a group; verb agreement varies by variety of English.

Compound

research project, break-in — two or more words functioning as a single noun.

Nominalisation

investigate → investigation — a verb or adjective converted into a noun; explored fully under Conversion.

Noun functions

04 Pronouns

Pronouns

Pronouns substitute for a noun or noun phrase, typically pointing back to something already mentioned (or about to be) rather than naming it directly.

CategoryExamples
PersonalI, you, she, we, them
Possessivemine, yours, hers, ours
Reflexivemyself, yourselves, itself
Demonstrativethis, that, these, those
Interrogativewho, whom, which, what
Relativewho, which, that, whose
Indefinitesomeone, anything, each, none

Dummy or expletive pronouns fill an obligatory grammatical slot (subject position) without referring to anything in the world — a purely structural, not referential, function.

05 Verbs

Verbs

The verb phrase is the engine of the clause: it carries tense, aspect, modality and voice, and determines how many other elements (objects, complements) the clause requires.

Lexical vs auxiliary

Lexical verbs carry the main meaning (analyse, believe). Auxiliary verbs support them grammatically: primary auxiliaries (be, have, do) and modal auxiliaries (can, must, might).

Transitive vs intransitive

Transitive verbs take a direct object (analysed the data); intransitive verbs do not (the data arrived).

Linking / copular

be, seem, become link a subject to a complement rather than an object: The results seem conclusive.

Finite vs non-finite

Finite verbs are marked for tense and agree with a subject; non-finite forms (infinitives, participles, gerunds) are not.

Phrasal verbs (set up, look into) combine a lexical verb with a particle to form a new, often idiomatic, unit.

Tense, aspect, modality, voice

Rather than a full tense course, note simply that the English verb phrase can layer several systems at once: tense (time reference), aspect (completion/duration), modality (necessity, possibility) and voice (active/passive) — each realised by auxiliaries and verb forms working together.

06 Adjectives

Adjectives

Adjectives qualify nouns and noun phrases, and can occupy two structurally distinct positions with different grammatical consequences.

ATTRIBUTIVE

a remarkably effective solution

remarkably = modifier · effective = adjective head · solution = noun

PREDICATIVE

The solution is effective.

Here the adjective follows a linking verb and functions as the subject complement.

Gradable / non-gradable

cold (very cold) vs dead (utterly dead, not very dead).

Participial adjectives

boring (causes) vs bored (experiences).

Comparative / superlative

more effective, most effective — treated in full on the dedicated Adjectives module.

Adjective phrases can also take a complement: proud of her achievement, eager that you should go.

07 Adverbs

Adverbs

'Adverb' is a broad, functionally diverse category — the classes below share a form label but do quite different jobs.

FunctionExample
Mannercarefully

Frequencyoften, rarely
Degreeextremely, barely
Viewpointgeographically speaking
Stanceprobably, frankly
Focusingonly, even, just
Linking / discoursehowever, therefore

Placement changes meaning

Focusing adverbs such as only attach to whatever immediately follows them — moving the adverb changes what is being restricted.

Only John criticised the proposal.

Nobody else criticised it — John was the sole critic.

08 Prepositions

Prepositions

Prepositions head a prepositional phrase and establish a relationship — spatial, temporal or abstract — between their complement and the rest of the sentence.

in the laboratory  ·  after the lecture  ·  with considerable confidence  ·  because of the evidence

Simple prepositions (in, after, with) are single words; complex prepositions (because of, in spite of, according to) are multi-word units functioning as one.

09 Conjunctions

Conjunctions

Conjunctions link words, phrases or clauses, and signal the logical relationship between what precedes and what follows.

COORDINATION

The evidence was limited, but the conclusion was persuasive.

Joins two independent clauses of equal grammatical status.

SUBORDINATION

Although the evidence was limited, the conclusion was persuasive.

Makes one clause dependent on the other — it cannot stand alone.

10 Interjections

Interjections

Interjections express emotion, reaction or social response, and stand largely outside the clause structure that governs the other word classes.

Oh!  ·  Wow!  ·  Well!  ·  Ah!  ·  Oops!

Interjections typically function as a complete utterance in their own right, common in spoken discourse, and are not integrated into surrounding clause structure the way other word classes are — they do not take a subject, object or modifier of their own. Their meaning is heavily dependent on intonation and context: Well can signal resignation, surprise or a pause for thought, depending on how and where it is used.

11 Word-Class Ambiguity

Same word, different class

Many English words belong to more than one word class, with identical spelling and pronunciation. The only way to classify them correctly is to examine the sentence they occur in.

I recorded the lecture.

The record was incomplete.

verb → noun

The room is light.

Please light the candle.

adjective → verb

He runs fast.

The fast train arrived.

adverb → adjective

The question to ask is never simply “what is this word?” but “what is this word doing here?”

12 Conversion

Conversion and word formation

English readily allows a word to shift word class without any change in form — a process called conversion or zero derivation — alongside more visible derivational processes that change a word's ending.

Zero derivation

email → to email  ·  text → to text  ·  Google → to google

No suffix is added and no spelling changes; only the syntactic context (a determiner before it, an inflection after it) reveals the new class. This differs from cases where morphology genuinely changes, covered next.

These suffixes are strong tendencies, not guarantees: friendly ends in -ly but is an adjective, and family ends in -ly but is a noun. Always confirm with the six-question framework rather than the suffix alone.

Academic English: nominalisation

Converting a verb into a related noun (nominalisation) is one of the most powerful stylistic resources in academic writing.

The researchers investigated the issue. → The investigation of the issue…

Nominalisation increases information density, allows the new noun phrase to be modified and embedded, and shifts emphasis away from a named human agent — effects associated with a more formal, abstract register. It does not automatically make writing ‘better’: overused, it can also make prose dense and impersonal. The right choice depends on purpose and audience.

Connection to IELTS

Confident, flexible control of word class supports several assessed dimensions of writing and speaking: lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, sentence variety, precision and cohesion. Recognising a word's class is not itself a scoring criterion, and no single grammatical feature guarantees a particular band — but the underlying flexibility it reflects is exactly what higher bands reward.
