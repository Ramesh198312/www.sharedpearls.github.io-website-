Foundational Linguistics: Active vs. Passive Voice Dynamics

Grammatical voice describes the semantic relationship between the verb, the subject performing the action (Agent), and the entity receiving or undergoing the action (Patient/Theme).

1. Information Flow & Prominence

Active Voice: Emphasizes the Agent (e.g., "The researcher analyzed the data"). It is direct, vigorous, and action-oriented.

Passive Voice: Elevates the Patient/Result to subject position (e.g., "The data was analyzed"). Essential in academic, legal, and scientific writing where the methodology or outcome supersedes the performer.

2. Transitivity Constraints

Only transitive verbs (verbs that take a direct object) can be transformed into the passive voice. Intransitive verbs (arrive, occur, disappear, sleep, happen) cannot form passives because there is no direct object to promote to subject position.

3. The By-Phrase Agent

In passive structures, the agent is either omitted entirely (when unknown, irrelevant, or obvious) or placed in a terminal prepositional phrase: by + agent (e.g., "...was ratified by parliament").

4. Continuous Perfect Constraints

While theoretical formulas exist for continuous perfect passives ("has been being tested"), natural English style avoids triple-auxiliary stacking (BEEN BEING) and substitutes the simple perfect passive instead.

1. Present Simple (Active & Passive)

Active Voice Formula
Subject + Base Verb (V1) (+ s/es)

"The senior committee evaluates every proposal."

Passive Voice Formula
Subject + is / am / are + Past Participle (V3)

"Every proposal is evaluated by the senior committee."

Linguistic Note: Present Simple describes timeless facts, scientific laws, habits, and scheduled realities. In the passive voice, it transfers prominence from the performer to the recipient of the action or the process itself.

Subject + is / am / are + being + Past Participle (V3)
