Division A

What a sentence constituent is

A sentence can be read as a string of units — blocks of language in a certain order. Each block is a sentence constituent, and it may be a single word or a whole phrase. Swap the words inside a block and the meaning changes; the order of the blocks stays the same. This useful but deliberately simplified picture is often called a slot and filler view of language.

Grammars do not agree on the labels. Different traditions classify constituents differently, and the descriptions can become very technical. The labels matter less than the two questions behind them: how are these units built, and how do they fit together?

Terminology varies between grammatical traditions. The terms used here follow the source chapter; where an alternative is common (transitive for object verb, copular for complement verb) it is named alongside.

Sentences, clauses, constituents

In writing we recognise a sentence by its capital letter and full stop. In speech the boundaries are far less obvious, and even in writing a long stretch can often be left whole or cut into shorter sentences.

Clauses are different: they can be identified without ambiguity in speech and in writing. This module deals with main clauses, which it simply calls clauses.

Constituents outside the clause

Divide a sentence into clauses and some material is left over. In I'll ring you on Friday but all of us will be keeping our fingers crossed, the coordinating conjunction but is a constituent of the sentence, yet it belongs to neither clause.

Such constituents either link clauses (and, but, or) or comment on the information and relate it to what came before.

Words versus phrases

The word phrase is used for a type of constituent, not for a minimum length. A noun phrase may be a single pronoun (us), a name (Bolivia), or twelve words with a relative clause hanging off the headword.

That is why She and The woman I told you about last Thursday occupy exactly the same slot.

Lab 05

Information flow

Stretches of language in which everything is new and important are hard to follow. Speakers and writers therefore balance the familiar against the new: familiar material first, to orientate the listener, and the new or important point later. The further towards the end of the clause something falls, the more attention it attracts.

Division B

When English moves beyond the basic pattern

Learners who are still building the basic order often want to secure it before experimenting. Yet anyone meeting real English meets the variants at once — and questions cannot be postponed at all, since they are needed from the first lessons.

Three things can be done with information: bring it forward, push it back, or put it under a spotlight. Every structure in this division is one of those three moves, plus the grammatical adjustments each move requires.

The three moves

Forward Fronting — adverbials, complements, direct objects; inversion after negative and limiting adverbials.
Back Pushing back — dummy subject there, preparatory it.
Spotlight Focusing — clefting and pseudo-clefting.

Subject question or object question?

The two look alike and mean something quite different. In an object question the question word asks about the object, and do / does / did is added. In a subject question the question word is the subject, and the word order of the statement is left alone.

Fronting is ordinary English, not decoration. We front material either because it — and not the subject — establishes common ground and orientates the reader, or because an unexpected position gives it extra prominence.

Lab 13

Adverbial position simulator

Adverbials are usually taught at the end of the clause, and often fronted. In fact they turn up in several intermediate positions too — after the verb phrase or inside it. "Adverbials always go at the end" is a convenient teaching simplification, not a rule of English.

Lab 14

Fronted complements and direct objects

Complement + subject + verb belongs largely to short, pithy spoken remarks and exclamations. Fronted direct objects occur mainly in speech. Neither swaps freely with the basic pattern: judge each one in its context.

Application

Constituents in speaking and writing

With a few exceptions, the variants rarely cause errors. The commoner and quieter problem is that learners play safe: from fear of mistakes, or simply from not knowing the means, they miss chances to spotlight, emphasise or play down part of what they are saying. It is in feedback on writing that this most often needs naming.
