Modal Auxiliary Verbs

Small verbs. Powerful meanings.

Modal auxiliaries are the handful of verbs — will, shall, can, could, may, might, must, should, would and their partners have to, be able to, ought to, had better, need — that let a speaker do far more than report a fact. With one short word you can signal ability, possibility, certainty, necessity, obligation, permission, advice, willingness, politeness and imagined worlds.

This course treats them as one connected system: what each form means, how it is built, when it is appropriate, how it changes when you talk about the past, and where learners most often go wrong. Every rule is followed immediately by two short checks.

Overview

How this course is built

Modal verbs are usually taught as a list. They are easier to learn as a meaning system: a small set of verbs that each cover several jobs, and several verbs that compete for the same job. This course moves through the jobs.

What makes a modal a modal

  • It is followed by the base form of the verb: She may leave. — never may to leave.
  • It never takes -s in the third person: He must go, not He musts go.
  • It makes questions and negatives on its own, with no do: Could you help? — She couldn’t help.
  • Only one modal per verb phrase. For a second meaning, use a partner form: will can → will be able to.

The partner forms

Modals have no infinitive and no participles, so English borrows ordinary verbs when a missing form is needed:

  • can → be able to (I’d like to be able to swim.)
  • must → have to (We’ll have to leave early.)
  • should → ought to (same meaning, keeps to)
  • Urgency → had better; lack of necessity → needn’t / don’t need to

Module group 1

Will, shall and future meaning

Will is the verb of the moment of speaking: decisions taken as you speak, offers, promises, refusals and predictions. What it does not do is announce arrangements you made earlier.

Module group 2

Ability and possibility: can, could, be able to

Can has only two forms, so English keeps be able to in reserve for every tense can cannot reach. And in the past, general ability and a single successful act are not expressed in the same way.

Module group 3

Possibility: may, might, could

These three say “perhaps”. The differences are small but real, and the negatives behave in a way that surprises most learners.

Module group 4

Deduction: must and can’t

Here must has nothing to do with obligation. It is the verb of a logical conclusion drawn from evidence — and can’t, not mustn’t, is its opposite.

Module group 5

Obligation, prohibition and free choice

The single most expensive mistake in this whole area is treating mustn’t and don’t have to as the same thing. One closes a door; the other opens it.

Module group 6

Advice, expectation and urgency

Should is the everyday advice verb, but it also carries expectation. Ought to shadows it closely; had better adds a warning that should never has.

Module group 7

Requests, offers, permission and invitations

Functional language, where grammar meets social distance. The past-tense forms (could, would) are not about past time at all — they buy politeness.

Module group 8

Past modals — the Modal Time Machine

Modals have no past tense of their own. To move a modal meaning into past time, English keeps the modal and changes what follows it: modal + have + past participle.

Module group 9

Modals in hypothetical and unreal situations

An advanced extension of the same system. When a speaker steps out of the real world, the modal set narrows to would, could and might — and the verb before them shifts one step back in time.

How to read evidence

Strong evidence that leaves no other explanation → must. Evidence that rules the idea out → can’t. Evidence that leaves the question open → may / might / could.
