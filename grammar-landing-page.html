A1–A2

Word order and clause elements

A typical statement puts the subject before the verb. A verb may require an object or a complement. Time and place expressions can add information about the situation.

Examples and usage tip
  • The children (subject) opened (verb) the window (object).
  • The soup (subject) tastes (linking verb) salty (subject complement).

In “The room became quiet”, quiet describes the subject. It is a complement.

A1–A2

Nouns, articles and quantity

A singular count noun typically needs a determiner. Use a or an for an indefinite singular count noun, and the when the listener can identify the referent. The choice of a or an depends on sound.

Examples and usage tip
  • I saw a fox. The fox ran across the road.
  • She brought an umbrella and some useful advice.

Use “a piece of advice” for one item of advice.

A1–A2

Pronouns and reference

Subject forms include I, she and they. Object forms such as me, her and them follow verbs or prepositions. A pronoun should have a clear referent in its context.

Examples and usage tip
  • She met him after work.
  • I made myself a cup of tea.

When two people could be the referent of she, repeat the relevant name.

A1–A2

Agreement, questions and negatives

With most lexical verbs, use do or does in present-simple questions and negatives. The main verb then takes its base form. With be as the finite verb, invert be and the subject in questions.

Examples and usage tip
  • Does Priya work on Fridays?
  • The children aren’t ready. Are they ready yet?

Edit “Does she works here?” to “Does she work here?”

A1–A2

Adjectives, adverbs and comparison

Adjectives can modify nouns or follow linking verbs. Adverbs can modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs or clauses. Frequent short adjectives often take -er, while longer adjectives commonly take more.

Examples and usage tip
  • It was a quiet evening. The room felt quiet.
  • Kemi spoke quietly, but her brother spoke more quietly.

Fast can be an adjective (“a fast train”) or an adverb (“it moves fast”).

A1–A2

Prepositions of time and place

Common time patterns include at a clock time, on a day and in a month or year. Place prepositions express different perspectives, such as location at a point or movement into an area.

Examples and usage tip
  • Meet me at six on Tuesday.
  • They waited at the station and then walked into the café.

Learn dependent prepositions as phrases, such as “interested in” and “depend on”.

A1–A2

Present and past verb forms

Use the present simple for routines and general statements. Use the present progressive for a situation in progress around now. The past simple can place an event at a finished past time.

Examples and usage tip
  • She usually walks to work, but today she’s taking the bus.
  • Yesterday she took the train.

Learn common irregular pairs such as go → went and take → took.

B1–B2

Tense, aspect and future reference

Tense locates a situation grammatically. Aspect shapes how it is presented. The progressive gives an internal view of an unfolding situation, and the present perfect connects an earlier situation to the present.

Examples and usage tip
  • I worked in Leeds in 2022. I’ve worked in Leeds since 2022.
  • The train leaves at nine tomorrow. We’re meeting at the station at eight.

In standard British English, use the past simple with a finished time such as yesterday.

B1–B2

Modals and degrees of certainty

Modal auxiliaries express meanings such as possibility, inference, permission and obligation. A single modal can convey different meanings in different contexts. The following verb takes its base form.

Examples and usage tip
  • You must wear a helmet. (obligation)
  • You must be tired after the journey. (strong inference)

“You mustn’t leave” prohibits leaving. “You don’t have to leave” removes the obligation.

B1–B2

Verb complementation

A verb’s sense helps determine which complements it accepts. Some verbs take an -ing clause, others a to-infinitive, and some allow both with a change of meaning.

Examples and usage tip
  • We avoided driving in the snow.
  • She stopped to answer a call. She stopped answering calls after six.

Use “suggest taking the bus” or “suggest that we take the bus”.

B1–B2

Relative clauses

A defining relative clause identifies the referent. A supplementary relative clause adds information and is normally set off by commas. You can often omit a defining relative marker when it functions as the object.

Examples and usage tip
  • The book (that) I lent you is on the desk.
  • My bike, which I bought last week, needs a new tyre.

Use who or which in a supplementary relative clause. Leave the relative marker in place.

B1–B2

Conditionals and imagined situations

The verb forms signal how the speaker frames the condition. An open condition treats the situation as a live possibility. A remote condition presents it as hypothetical or unreal.

Examples and usage tip
  • If it rains, we’ll stay inside.
  • If I lived closer, I would walk. If I had left earlier, I would have caught the train.

A past form in “If I lived closer” expresses remoteness about the present situation.

B1–B2

Active and passive voice

The passive generally uses be plus a past participle. The active object commonly becomes the passive subject. Choose the passive when that participant is the topic or the agent is unknown or unimportant.

Examples and usage tip
  • The council repaired the bridge in May.
  • The bridge was repaired in May. The work was completed by local engineers.

Choosing the passive changes what the sentence starts with and may leave the agent implicit.

B1–B2

Reported speech and questions

Reporting re-expresses earlier speech from a new viewpoint. Backshift is common with past reporting verbs and may be unnecessary when a fact still holds. Embedded questions use statement order.

Examples and usage tip
  • “I’m tired.” → She said she was tired.
  • “Where is the station?” → He asked where the station was.

Edit “She asked where was I going” to “She asked where I was going”.

B1–B2

Multi-word verbs

A particle can form a lexical unit with a verb. Some transitive phrasal verbs allow the object between the verb and particle. With a separable verb, an unstressed personal pronoun normally goes before the particle.

Examples and usage tip
  • Turn off the light. Turn the light off. Turn it off.
  • We looked after the children. We looked after them.

Learn “look after someone” as a unit. After remains before its complement.

C1–C2

Information focus, inversion and clefts

English can rearrange information to control emphasis. After certain fronted negative expressions, put the auxiliary before the subject. Clefts create a focus position.

Examples and usage tip
  • Rarely have we seen such a rapid change.
  • It was the opening paragraph that confused readers.

Use “Only after the review did we publish”, with inversion in the main clause.

C1–C2

Subordination, cohesion and ellipsis

Subordinate clauses express relations such as cause, concession and purpose. Cohesion also comes from reference, lexical repetition and the omission of material that readers can recover.

Examples and usage tip
  • Although the sample was small, the pattern was consistent.
  • Amira rewrote the introduction, and Joel did too.

Use a clear referent for “this”, such as “this change”, when a bare pronoun may be ambiguous.

C1–C2

Punctuation, attachment and register

Punctuation can signal clause boundaries and guide interpretation. Place modifiers near the element they describe. In formal writing, make abstract relations and ambiguous references explicit.

Examples and usage tip
  • After reviewing the figures, the team revised the estimate.
  • The estimate rose. However, demand remained steady.

In “After reviewing the figures, the estimate was revised”, the estimate cannot be the reviewer. Name the reviewer as the main-clause subject.

C1–C2

Subjunctive and reporting verbs

A mandative clause expresses a recommendation, demand or requirement. Formal English allows the base form of the verb, and British English also commonly uses should plus the base form.

Examples and usage tip
  • They recommended that he be present.
  • We asked that the report should remain confidential.

Check whether “insisted” introduces a demand (“that she leave”) or a claim (“that she had left”).

Word order and clause elementsA1–A2

How it works

Find the finite verb first and identify its subject. Then ask which further elements the verb selects. Give commonly takes a recipient and a thing given. Put commonly needs a location as well as an object. Optional information such as yesterday usually works as an adjunct.

Compare the examples

“Ellie gave Tom the key yesterday” has two objects, Tom and the key. In “Ellie put the key in her pocket”, the location completes the ordinary placement sense of put.

Try it

Analyse “The technician put the samples in the fridge after lunch”. Identify the subject, verb, object, location and time adjunct.

Reveal a model answer

The technician is the subject, put is the verb, the samples is the object, in the fridge supplies the location required by put, and after lunch adds the time.

Nouns, articles and quantityA1–A2

How it works

Choose a determiner after deciding whether a noun is countable in the intended sense and whether the listener can identify what you mean. A plural or mass noun can appear without an article when you are speaking generally. Some nouns alternate between mass and count uses.

Compare the examples

“We bought paper” means the material. “We read a paper” can mean a particular article. “The paper on your desk” identifies a specific item.

Try it

Complete “I need ___ information about ___ bus that leaves at six.” Use some, a or the.

Reveal a model answer

Use “some information” because information is a mass noun and “the bus” because the departure time identifies it.

Pronouns and referenceA1–A2

How it works

Check pronoun form and reference separately. Use a subject form for the subject of a finite clause and an object form after most prepositions. A reflexive can mark an object that refers back to the same subject. Singular they takes the usual they agreement, as in “They are”.

Compare the examples

“Sam introduced himself” means Sam introduced Sam. “Sam introduced him” normally points to another person in that context.

Try it

Rewrite “Everyone should bring his or her notebook. He or she can keep it on the desk” using singular they.

Reveal a model answer

“Everyone should bring their notebook. They can keep it on the desk.” Their and they refer back to the individual picked out by everyone.

Agreement, questions and negativesA1–A2

How it works

Identify the head of the subject phrase before choosing agreement. In “the lid of the jars”, the head is lid. In a present or past question with a lexical verb and no other auxiliary, add do, does or did and put the subject after it.

Compare the examples

“The lid of the jars needs replacing” has a singular subject. “Did the lids need replacing?” places past marking on did and uses need in its base form.

Try it

Turn “The new student understands the instructions” into a question and a negative statement.

Reveal a model answer

“Does the new student understand the instructions?” and “The new student doesn’t understand the instructions.” Does carries the present-tense agreement in both.

Adjectives, adverbs and comparisonA1–A2

How it works

Separate the word class from its job. An adjective can modify a noun or describe the subject after a linking verb. A manner adverb tells you how something is done. Common comparison patterns include faster than, more carefully than and as carefully as.

Compare the examples

“The bread smells fresh” describes the bread. “She sliced it carefully” describes the action. “This knife is sharper than that one” compares two knives.

Try it

Edit “The new machine works more quiet than the old one”.

Reveal a model answer

“The new machine works more quietly than the old one.” Quietly describes how the machine works.

Prepositions of time and placeA1–A2

How it works

Prepositions often express a relation between a situation and a reference point. Use by for a deadline and until for continuation up to a time. To marks movement towards a destination, while at commonly identifies a point or place associated with an activity.

Compare the examples

“Send the form by Friday” allows earlier submission. “The office stays open until Friday” describes a state that continues up to Friday.

Try it

Choose by or until in each gap. “The library stays open ___ eight. Please return the keys ___ eight.”

Reveal a model answer

Use until for the period of opening and by for the latest acceptable return time.

Present and past verb formsA1–A2

How it works

Start from the intended time or situation. The present simple works for routines and states. The present progressive often describes temporary activity around now. The past simple normally describes a past event or state in a finished time frame. Many verbs that describe states, such as know, normally use the simple form.

Compare the examples

“Nadia lives in York” presents a current situation as stable. “She’s staying in Glasgow this week” presents a temporary arrangement. “She stayed there last spring” gives a finished past time.

Try it

Choose a form of work for each gap. “He usually ___ from home. This week he ___ at the branch. Yesterday he ___ until six.”

Reveal a model answer

Use works, is working and worked. The cues point to a routine, a temporary situation and a finished past day respectively.

Return to the topic summary

Tense, aspect and future referenceB1–B2

How it works

Map events onto the time you are speaking about. Use the past perfect when an earlier event matters to a past reference point. The present perfect looks back from the present. For future reference, compare a timetable in the present simple with an arrangement in the present progressive.

Compare the examples

“When we arrived, the film had started” puts the start first. “When we arrived, the film started” usually places the start at or just after arrival.

Try it

Explain the difference between “I’ve worked on the report all morning” and “I worked on the report all morning”.

Reveal a model answer

The present perfect presents the morning or the work in relation to now. The past simple presents the morning as a finished past period.

Modals and degrees of certaintyB1–B2

How it works

Identify the source of the modal meaning. A rule can impose an obligation, and evidence can support an inference. Use modal plus have and a past participle to reason about an earlier event. The strength of a modal depends on the context.

Compare the examples

“Visitors must sign in” sets a requirement. “She must have left early. Her desk is empty” presents a strong inference about the past.

Try it

Rewrite “Perhaps Maya missed the train” using a modal and preserving the uncertainty.

Reveal a model answer

“Maya may have missed the train.” May signals possibility, and have missed places it before the time of speaking.

Verb complementationB1–B2

How it works

Learn recurring patterns together with the verb and its sense. Expect takes an object and to-infinitive in “We expected them to arrive”. Make takes a bare infinitive after the object in an active clause. With remember, the complement changes the time relationship.

Compare the examples

“I remembered to lock the door” means the remembering came first. “I remember locking the door” means I recall an earlier action.

Try it

In a reminder about a task that still needs doing, choose the form. “Remember locking / to lock the gate before you leave.”

Reveal a model answer

Use “Remember to lock the gate”. The reminder is about a future action.

Relative clausesB1–B2

How it works

Decide whether the clause identifies which person or thing you mean or adds a comment about an already identified referent. A defining clause normally has no enclosing commas. In a defining relative clause, omission is possible for an object gap, but keep the relative marker when it is the subject.

Compare the examples

“The singer we heard was excellent” can omit the object relative marker. “The singer who opened the concert was excellent” keeps who as the subject of opened.

Try it

Restore the commas if there is only one relevant printer. “The office printer which was repaired yesterday is making a noise again.”

Reveal a model answer

“The office printer, which was repaired yesterday, is making a noise again.” Its repair is additional information about an already identified printer.

Conditionals and imagined situationsB1–B2

How it works

Choose verb forms by time and by the speaker’s stance. A present form can introduce an open future condition. Past forms can frame a hypothetical present or future. The past perfect can frame an unreal past condition. A mixed conditional can relate a past condition to a present consequence.

Compare the examples

“If you see Ella, tell her I called” is open. “If I had accepted the job, I would be living abroad now” combines an unreal past decision with an imagined present result.

Try it

Combine “I didn’t bring my charger. My laptop is unusable now” into a mixed conditional.

Reveal a model answer

“If I had brought my charger, my laptop would be usable now.” Had brought sets the unreal past condition, while would be describes the imagined current result.

Active and passive voiceB1–B2

How it works

When rewriting in the passive, preserve the tense and aspect of the original. The auxiliary be receives the relevant forms, and the lexical verb takes its past participle. Choose between active and passive by deciding what belongs in subject position and whether the agent matters.

Compare the examples

“Staff are checking applications” becomes “Applications are being checked”. “Staff have checked applications” becomes “Applications have been checked”.

Try it

Rewrite “The engineers will inspect the tunnels tomorrow” with the tunnels as the subject and the agent omitted.

Reveal a model answer

“The tunnels will be inspected tomorrow.” Will is retained and be inspected forms the passive.

Reported speech and questionsB1–B2

How it works

Adapt person, place and time expressions to the reporting context. Backshift often puts the reported event in the past reporting frame, but a speaker can retain a present form for information that is still current. Embedded yes/no questions normally begin with if or whether.

Compare the examples

“Do you have a ticket?” can become “She asked whether I had a ticket”. “I work here” could become “He says he works there” when reported from another place.

Try it

Report “Will you call me tomorrow?” from the listener’s viewpoint two days later.

Reveal a model answer

“She asked whether I would call her the next day.” The pronouns change with viewpoint, and would and the next day fit the past report.

Multi-word verbsB1–B2

How it works

Learn the whole construction, its meaning and its object pattern. An everyday physical verb can acquire an idiomatic meaning with a particle. A prepositional verb keeps its complement after the preposition. A three-part pattern such as put up with also keeps the complement after its final preposition.

Compare the examples

“We put the meeting off” means we postponed it. “We put up with the noise” means we tolerated it. Say “put it off” and “put up with it”.

Try it

Replace “the radio” with it in “Please switch off the radio”. Then do the same for “Please deal with the complaint”.

Reveal a model answer

Use “Please switch it off” and “Please deal with it”. Switch off permits the pronoun before its particle, while with keeps its complement after it.

Information focus, inversion and cleftsC1–C2

How it works

Find the element you want the reader to focus on. A cleft can contrast that element with an alternative. Fronted restrictive or negative expressions can trigger subject–auxiliary inversion in a formal style. If the clause has no auxiliary, add the appropriate form of do.

Compare the examples

“It was on Friday that we signed” foregrounds the date. “Only then did we sign” gives prominence to the time restriction.

Try it

Rewrite “We recognised the error only after checking the totals” with “Only after checking the totals” first.

Reveal a model answer

“Only after checking the totals did we recognise the error.” The main clause uses did before we, and recognise takes the base form.

Return to the topic summary

Subordination, cohesion and ellipsisC1–C2

How it works

Make the relation between clauses explicit before choosing a linker. Although introduces a concession inside a sentence. However can connect ideas across independent sentences. In coordinated clauses, omission works when the missing words are easy to recover and the subjects remain clear.

Compare the examples

“Although the route was shorter, it took longer” expresses concession. “Mina reviewed the figures, and Leo the invoices” leaves reviewed recoverable from the first clause.

Try it

Join “The route was shorter. It took longer.” using although. Then join it using however.

Reveal a model answer

“Although the route was shorter, it took longer.” Alternatively, “The route was shorter. However, it took longer.” Each version signals a contrast and uses a different clause structure.

Punctuation, attachment and registerC1–C2

How it works

Read a sentence for structure before editing punctuation. A comma can follow a fronted subordinate clause or enclose supplementary information. Two independent clauses can be joined by a full stop, a semicolon or a coordinating conjunction. Check that opening modifiers clearly connect to a plausible subject.

Compare the examples

“The first trial failed. The second succeeded.” Each clause can stand alone. “After reviewing the data, we repeated the trial” makes we the understood reviewer.

Try it

Edit “After inspecting the roof, the leak was obvious, we called a builder.” Keep the sequence of events clear.

Reveal a model answer

“After inspecting the roof, we spotted the leak and called a builder.” The subject we gives the modifier an agent, and the coordinator joins the two actions.

Subjunctive and reporting verbsC1–C2

How it works

Read the reporting verb in context. A verb such as insist can report a demand or a claim. After a demand, formal English permits the base-form subjunctive, including be and negative not plus the base form. A report of a factual claim ordinarily uses an indicative form.

Compare the examples

“They insisted that the door be locked” presents a demand. “They insisted that the door was locked” can report their claim about the door’s state.

Try it

Make the intended demand explicit in “The chair insisted that the figures were kept confidential.”

Reveal a model answer

“The chair insisted that the figures be kept confidential.” In British English, “should be kept confidential” is another way to express the demand.

Editing clinic

Decide what needs changing in each sentence before revealing a revision.

Agreement

The list of changes are on the desk.

Reveal a revision

The list of changes is on the desk.

The head of the subject phrase is the singular noun list. Changes belongs to the modifying phrase of changes.

Modifier attachment

Walking to the station, the rain began.

Reveal a revision

As we walked to the station, the rain began.

The finite subordinate clause names who was walking and keeps the intended main-clause subject.

Reference

When Riya called Lena, she was already at the station.

Reveal a revision

When Riya called Lena, Lena was already at the station.

If Lena was at the station, name her explicitly. Depending on the intended meaning, she could refer to either person.
