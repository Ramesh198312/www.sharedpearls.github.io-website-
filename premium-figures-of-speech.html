Figures of Speech

How Great Writers Paint Pictures with Words

Figures of speech are not just decorative frills. They help us connect different ideas, express powerful emotions, add musical rhythm to sentences, and make everyday language unforgettable.

01 Overview

Why We Use Figures of Speech

A figure of speech is an imaginative use of words that goes beyond their plain, literal dictionary meaning to produce a striking impression on the reader or listener.

Good writers turn to figurative language whenever literal words feel too flat or dry. In classical rhetoric, these devices fall into two broad groups: Tropes (which turn words away from their ordinary meaning, like metaphors and irony) and Schemes (which arrange words in artful rhythms and balanced patterns, like alliteration and antithesis). When learners master these devices, their writing moves from basic descriptive sentences into lively, confident English.

The Six-Question Reading Check

Whenever you examine a striking line of poetry or prose, ask these six simple questions:

QUESTION 1

Which device or pattern is being used?

QUESTION 2

What is the real subject being described?

QUESTION 3

What picture or lens is the writer holding up?

QUESTION 4

What shared quality connects the two things?

QUESTION 5

What feeling or mood does this contrast create?

QUESTION 6

How does it change the tone of the passage?

Helpful tip: Authors frequently layer several figures together: The pale street swallowed our frantic footsteps like water. Here, personification (the street swallowing), metaphor (footsteps as food), and simile (like water) work together in a single breath.

03 Comparison & Imagery

Simile & Metaphor

The two workhorses of creative writing: linking two separate things with a connecting word, or boldly saying one thing is another.

SIMILE • USING LIKE OR AS

"O my Luve is like a red, red rose / That's newly sprung in June."

Uses connecting words (like, as, than) to set two separate ideas side by side while keeping their difference clear.

METAPHOR • DIRECT MATCH

"All the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players."

Drops "like" and "as" to state an immediate match: the real world becomes an actual theatrical stage in the mind of the reader.

04 Giving Life to the Inanimate

Personification & Apostrophe

Treating weather, objects, and abstract ideas as living people, or calling out directly to an absent friend or nature itself.

Personification

"Because I could not stop for Death — / He kindly stopped for me —"

Emily Dickinson imagines death not as a terrifying monster, but as a polite, patient gentleman driving a horse carriage.

Apostrophe

"O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn's being, / Hear, oh hear!"

Shelley turns away from his human audience to speak directly to the autumn wind as if it could hear and answer his plea.

05 Blowing Things Up & Playing Them Down

Hyperbole & Litotes

Dramatic exaggeration used for emotional power, contrasted with quiet understatement using a double negative.

HYPERBOLE • DRAMATIC EXAGGERATION

"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red."

Macbeth's guilt is so vast that he exaggerates a drop of blood into a crimson dye capable of turning all the oceans red.

LITOTES • CAREFUL UNDERSTATEMENT

"I am a citizen of no mean city."  •  "The twelve-hour flight was not entirely pleasant."

Saying "no mean city" is a modest, ironical way of saying an illustrious one; "not entirely pleasant" describes a harrowing journey with dry wit.

07 When Things Turn Upside Down

Verbal, Situational & Dramatic Irony

Irony happens whenever there is a sharp, meaningful gap between what is said and meant, or what is expected and what actually occurs.

Verbal Irony

"Yet Brutus says he was ambitious; / And Brutus is an honourable man."

Mark Antony says the opposite of what he actually believes to gently tear down Brutus's reputation before the Roman crowd.

Situational Irony

"Water, water, everywhere, / Nor any drop to drink."

A sailor surrounded by millions of gallons of ocean water dying of thirst. The situation mocks normal expectations.

Dramatic Irony

King Oedipus fiercely swears to track down and banish the man who killed King Laius.

The audience knows the tragic truth that Oedipus cannot yet see: he is the killer he is hunting.

08 Stand-Ins & Symbols

Metonymy & Synecdoche

Using a closely related item as a stand-in, or using a physical body part to stand for the whole person.

METONYMY • CONNECTED OBJECT

"The pen is mightier than the sword."  •  "The Crown issued a decree."

Uses an external symbol to stand for an entire institution (Pen = journalism and law; Sword = military force; Crown = royalty).

SYNECDOCHE • PART FOR THE WHOLE

"Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships?"  •  "All hands on deck!"

Names a real physical part (Helen's face; sailors' hands) to focus our immediate attention on that vivid detail.

09 Sound & Rhythm

Alliteration, Assonance & Sound Words

Using letter sounds, vowel echoes, and sound-effect words to set the pace and mood of a sentence.

Alliteration

"Deep into that darkness peering, long I stood there wondering, fearing, doubting, dreaming dreams..."

Poe repeats the tapping /d/ sound to create a rhythm of creeping fear and suspense.

Assonance

"Thou foster-child of silence and slow time."

Keats repeats long vowel sounds (/i/ and /o/) to deliberately slow down our reading speed.

Onomatopoeia

"The rusty iron gate groaned and screeched as it swung shut."

The words sound just like the physical squeal and grinding of old metal hinges.

10 Balanced Sentences

Antithesis, Chiasmus, Anaphora & Zeugma

Shaping sentences through balance, word repetition, criss-cross inversions, and playful double meanings.

ANTITHESIS (BALANCED OPPOSITES)

"That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind."

Balances two matching clauses to contrast one individual footstep against all of human progress.

CHIASMUS (CRISS-CROSS SENTENCES)

"Let us never negotiate out of fear (A-B), but let us never fear to negotiate (B-A)."

Flips the key words in reverse order like a mirror, giving the advice an authoritative, memorable ring.

ANAPHORA (STARTING REPETITIONS)

"We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields..."

Repeating the opening phrase again and again builds determination and power in a spoken speech.

ZEUGMA (PLAYFUL DOUBLE USE)

"She broke his vintage watch and his heart."

One single action verb ("broke") ties together a solid physical object (a watch) and an invisible emotional feeling (a heart).
