boycott · B2

verb and countable noun · Module 01

/ˈbɔɪkɒt/

To refuse to buy from, work with, or take part in something as a deliberate protest.

Origin. English. Irish Land War · County Mayo · Charles Cunningham Boycott.

Earlier sense. A name applied to a campaign of organised non-cooperation against an estate agent.

Current sense. A tactic used by customers, workers, organisations or governments to put pressure on a target.

History and meaning

In autumn 1880, a dispute at an estate near Lough Mask in County Mayo acquired a public life far beyond the district. Charles Cunningham Boycott managed property for Lord Erne. Tenants faced pressure over rents and the threat of eviction, and local organisers connected these grievances to a wider movement for land reform. Boycott became the focus of a campaign in which workers and people who provided everyday services withdrew their co-operation. The practical effect was immediate: running a farm requires many willing participants, from people who do the work to those who supply goods or carry messages.

The campaign took place during the Irish Land War, a period in which tenants challenged the power of landlords and agents over rents and holdings. Refusing an ordinary transaction could become a collective political act. Bringing in workers from elsewhere to harvest crops, with protection organised for them, demonstrated how difficult and expensive it was to replace local co-operation. Wider reporting turned a dispute on a particular estate into a recognisable example of a tactic that readers could understand and name.

The surname moved unusually quickly into English as a verb, and the noun came to describe the campaign itself. People had withdrawn trade and labour long before 1880. What changed was that the conspicuous Mayo case provided a name applicable to other disputes. A modern boycott can be local, such as a group declining to use a shop, or international, such as organisations refusing to participate in an event. The force of the word lies in organised or publicly signalled refusal. Whether the action succeeds depends on questions the word alone cannot answer, such as how many people join and whether the target can replace what it has lost.

Timeline

1879

The Irish National Land League forms against a background of rural distress.

1880

Workers and local businesses withdraw co-operation from the agent in County Mayo.

Late 1880

News reports rapidly spread the name as a term for the tactic.

Today

The term applies across political, commercial and cultural contexts.

Usage, pronunciation and eight examples

Common pairings. organise a boycott; call for a boycott; consumer boycott; boycott an event

Related language. boycotter; boycotting; related: embargo, strike, protest

Watch for this. A strike is a withdrawal of labour. A boycott can target a product, company or event. Simply disliking something does not amount to a boycott.

Say it aloud. Stress BOY. Contrast the /ɔɪ/ in boy with the /ɒ/ in cot. Then read two examples, retaining the same word stress.

  1. The tenants discussed whether to boycott the auction.
  2. Several retailers joined the boycott of the supplier.
  3. The organisers called for a boycott of the awards ceremony.
  4. Some fans ended their boycott after the club published the agreement.
  5. The union supported the boycott but did not call a strike.
  6. She said she was boycotting the product until its label changed.
  7. The planned boycott lost support when smaller traders raised concerns.
  8. A boycott can draw attention to an issue even if sales barely change.

Luddite · C1

countable noun · Module 03

/ˈlʌdaɪt/

Historically, a participant in early nineteenth-century protests by textile workers. In common modern use, a person perceived as resisting new technology.

Origin. English. Industrial England · textile work · Ned Ludd.

Earlier sense. A name attached to workers protesting changes in textile production.

Current sense. A sometimes dismissive, sometimes humorous label for reluctance to adopt technology.

History and meaning

The Luddites emerged from an industry in which skilled textile workers faced changes to pay, employment and the organisation of production. In Nottinghamshire in 1811, workers and their supporters challenged the way employers were using machinery. Frame breaking became the best remembered form of protest, but petitions, collective appeals and threats also formed part of the movement. The tools mattered because they could change who did the work, what counted as skill and how much a household could earn. A description of the protests that begins and ends with a dislike of machines leaves out what was at stake.

The name Ned Ludd appears in communications from the period. The National Archives preserves a proclamation issued in that name in December 1811. It threatens people who give information about frame breaking to the authorities. This is evidence that activists used the name. It does not establish that an individual called Ned Ludd led the protests. The figure probably operated as a shared identity, allowing people or groups to speak as if they belonged to one movement. The state responded severely. The movement spread into several manufacturing regions, and some people convicted in the repression were executed or transported.

Modern usage often compresses this history into a quick judgement about a person’s attitude to change. Someone who jokingly calls herself a Luddite may mean she prefers a paper diary to an app. The same label in a dispute about workplace software can imply that objections are irrational or backward. To read the word fairly, ask whether the dissenter objects to the tool, its costs, a lack of training or the employer’s way of implementing it. That distinction brings the historical and present meanings into a useful conversation.

Timeline

1811

Disturbances begin in Nottinghamshire and a proclamation appears in Ned Ludd’s name.

1812

The protests spread in parts of Yorkshire and Lancashire, and Parliament intensifies the penalties for machine-breaking.

1813

Seventeen men are executed following a special commission at York.

Today

The word is often used for perceived resistance to technology.

Usage, pronunciation and eight examples

Common pairings. label someone a Luddite; an avowed Luddite; Luddite protests; digital Luddite

Related language. Luddites; Luddism; related: technophobe, sceptic, early adopter

Watch for this. A person can object to the introduction of a tool for a specific reason without objecting to technology in general. Identify the period before choosing the sense.

Say it aloud. Stress LUD, with the vowel in cup. The second syllable rhymes with light. Then read two examples, retaining the same word stress.

  1. I call myself a Luddite whenever I lose another password.
  2. The report described the Luddites as skilled workers defending their livelihoods.
  3. He resented being called a Luddite for asking what the app would collect.
  4. Some colleagues joke that I am a Luddite because I still use a paper diary.
  5. The museum displayed a letter issued in the name of the Luddites.
  6. The article contrasted Luddite protests with later campaigns for workplace safety.
  7. The panel’s chair said that questioning a rollout did not make someone a Luddite.
  8. Historians disagree with accounts that reduce the Luddites to fear of change.

maverick · C1

countable noun and attributive adjective · Module 01

/ˈmævərɪk/

An independent-minded person who often acts outside the conventions of a group.

Origin. American English. Nineteenth-century Texas · Samuel Augustus Maverick.

Earlier sense. An unbranded animal, especially a calf, in the cattle-ranching vocabulary of the American West.

Current sense. A person whose decisions or methods are conspicuously independent of an institution or group.

History and meaning

In cattle country, a brand made claims of ownership legible. Once animals had dispersed, a mark could link an animal to a particular owner or herd. An animal without a visible mark was harder to place, and the uncertainty could matter in a business where livestock ranged across large areas. The English noun maverick is generally associated with the surname of Samuel Augustus Maverick, a nineteenth-century Texas landholder, lawyer and public figure. Historical accounts connect him with cattle that went unbranded and with the subsequent use of his surname for such animals.

It is worth separating this association from the more memorable stories told about it. Accounts differ in detail about how the herd was acquired and managed, and why it went unbranded. It is easy to construct a character portrait in which a man consciously rejects the conventions of ranching, but the fact that the English word now means an independent person does not establish the intentions of the man whose name it bears. The spelling and survival of the word are easier to observe than the exact conversation at which it supposedly began. The term is recorded in the nineteenth century and belongs to a wider ranching culture that already had practical reasons to distinguish marked and unmarked cattle.

The figurative step is productive. A person called a maverick remains recognisable as a member or participant in a community, but they do not easily fit one of its usual positions. A political maverick may vote against colleagues; a maverick architect may propose an unusual solution. The label can praise originality or warn about unpredictability. In a biography, an editor should make that judgement explicit by explaining the person’s actual choices.

Timeline

Mid-nineteenth century

Samuel Augustus Maverick is associated with an unbranded herd in Texas.

Later nineteenth century

The English cattle term is recorded in print.

Later use

The name becomes a figurative description of independent people.

Usage, pronunciation and eight examples

Common pairings. a political maverick; a maverick designer; a maverick approach; a reputation as a maverick

Related language. mavericks; related: nonconformist, independent thinker, outsider

Watch for this. Maverick describes independence and can express approval or criticism. A person can be a maverick within a group without being excluded from it.

Say it aloud. Three syllables. Stress MAV and keep the middle vowel short and weak. Then read two examples, retaining the same word stress.

  1. The board hired a maverick who had successfully challenged several industry conventions.
  2. She is a political maverick who rarely votes along party lines.
  3. The paper gave its maverick columnist considerable freedom.
  4. An unbranded calf was called a maverick in American ranching usage.
  5. The team admired his maverick ideas but wanted the safety checks documented.
  6. The producer had a reputation as a maverick long before her first hit.
  7. His latest plan seems cautious for a politician known as a maverick.
  8. A maverick approach can uncover assumptions that everyone else takes for granted.

Draconian · C1

adjective · Module 02

/drəˈkəʊniən/

So severe that a rule or penalty seems excessive.

Origin. Ancient Greek name, later English adjective. Archaic Athens · Draco · written law.

Earlier sense. Associated with Draco, an early Athenian lawgiver whose later reputation associated him with extremely harsh penalties.

Current sense. An evaluative adjective for rules, measures and punishments judged excessively strict.

History and meaning

Putting laws in writing can change who is able to appeal to them and what officials can claim the law says. In archaic Athens, Draco is associated with an early effort to write down law, traditionally placed towards the end of the seventh century BCE. What survives is uneven. An Athenian inscription from 409 or 408 BCE orders the republication of a law on homicide attributed to Draco. The stone records an act of republication centuries after the traditional date. It provides a firmer foundation than stories that enumerate dramatic punishments for minor theft.

Later writers gave Draco a reputation for extraordinary severity. Their stories deserve attention because they show how his name was understood, even when they cannot prove exactly what his rules said. The reputation and the surviving text answer different questions. The fragment can provide evidence about an Athenian procedure and its later preservation. A later story about savage punishment can show how subsequent generations explained Draco’s reputation. A reader should avoid using either to claim that we have a complete copy of his original code.

The English adjective carries the judgement transmitted by that reputation. It commonly describes a measure thought more severe than its purpose warrants. A newspaper that calls a policy Draconian is choosing a more critical word than strict. The speaker might be objecting to how many people the rule affects, how long a restriction lasts, or the relation between a mistake and its penalty. For example, a permanent ban for a minor first offence may invite that description. The crucial comparison is between the burden imposed and the reason offered. The word can open an argument about fairness, but the adjective itself cannot settle it.

Timeline

Traditionally 621/620 BCE

Draco’s laws are placed in archaic Athens.

409/408 BCE

Athens orders republication of his homicide law. A fragment of the inscription survives.

Later reception

Stories about severity establish Draco’s reputation.

Modern use

The adjective is applied to penalties and policies outside the ancient setting.

Usage, pronunciation and eight examples

Common pairings. Draconian measures; Draconian penalties; a Draconian ban; condemn a rule as Draconian

Related language. Draco; related: harsh, disproportionate, punitive; contrast: lenient

Watch for this. Draconian is a judgement. State the rule and explain why its penalty seems out of proportion.

Say it aloud. Stress CO, with /əʊ/ as in go. Keep the first vowel weak. Then read two examples, retaining the same word stress.

  1. Residents called the new parking fines Draconian.
  2. The panel rejected a Draconian ban in favour of written warnings.
  3. The rule was strict, but few staff thought it Draconian.
  4. Civil liberties groups described the restrictions as Draconian.
  5. A Draconian penalty can discourage people from reporting honest mistakes.
  6. The editorial explained why it considered the measures Draconian.
  7. The head teacher withdrew a proposal for Draconian punishments.
  8. A policy can look Draconian if it makes no allowance for exceptional circumstances.

Pyrrhic victory · C1

countable noun phrase · Module 02

/ˌpɪrɪk ˈvɪktəri/

A success whose costs largely cancel the advantages of winning.

Origin. Ancient Greek name, later English phrase. Epirus and Roman Italy · Pyrrhus · Asculum.

Earlier sense. A victory associated with Pyrrhus’s costly campaign against Rome.

Current sense. Any apparent success that undermines the person or group that achieved it.

History and meaning

Pyrrhus, king of Epirus, crossed to southern Italy in the early third century BCE to aid Tarentum against Rome. The contest that followed helps explain why an apparently successful battle can leave a commander in a worse strategic position. Pyrrhus’s army fought the Romans at Heraclea and then at Asculum, conventionally dated to 279 BCE. The name of the latter battle is particularly associated with the expression Pyrrhic victory. It was expensive in experienced soldiers and officers, people whose loss mattered for the campaigns still ahead.

One influential account is in Plutarch’s Life of Pyrrhus. Plutarch writes long after the campaign and gives readers more than one version of the events, including differences about the course and outcome of the fighting. He reports a remark in which Pyrrhus, hearing congratulations, points out that another victory of the same kind would ruin him. This is valuable evidence for the ancient tradition behind the expression. It should be presented as a reported remark, rather than as a transcript taken down at the battle. What makes the story intelligible is the contrast between the ability of Rome to replace personnel and the difficulty of Pyrrhus’s army in making good its losses.

In present English, the expression transfers this contrast to other kinds of competition. The first test is whether the party really got something it sought. The second is whether doing so undermined a deeper objective. A project might win approval while consuming so much money and goodwill that it can no longer be delivered. Or a team might win one match while losing key players for the tournament. The judgement depends on what the winner values and the period over which the consequences are measured.

Timeline

280 BCE

Pyrrhus engages Rome at Heraclea.

279 BCE

The armies fight at Asculum. Ancient accounts differ in detail.

Centuries later

Plutarch transmits the warning about another such victory.

Today

The phrase applies in politics, work and sport.

Usage, pronunciation and eight examples

Common pairings. win a Pyrrhic victory; prove a Pyrrhic victory; a potentially Pyrrhic victory

Related language. related: hollow victory, costly success; contrast: lasting success

Watch for this. An expensive success can still be worthwhile. Pyrrhic implies the costs undermine the gains. A defeat by itself does not qualify.

Say it aloud. Say Pyrrhic with two short /ɪ/ vowels. Put the strongest phrase stress on VIC in victory. Then read two examples, retaining the same word stress.

  1. The vote was a Pyrrhic victory that split the governing coalition.
  2. Winning the contract proved a Pyrrhic victory once the penalties came into effect.
  3. The captain feared a Pyrrhic victory after three players were injured.
  4. We kept our funding, but losing the partnership could make it a Pyrrhic victory.
  5. The shareholders called the takeover a Pyrrhic victory for the board.
  6. An impressive first sale can be a Pyrrhic victory if it exhausts the stock needed for regular customers.
  7. The union celebrated the agreement, although some members warned it might be a Pyrrhic victory.
  8. The report asks whether the short-term savings represent a Pyrrhic victory.

algorithm · B2

countable noun · Module 05

/ˈælɡərɪðəm/

A set of organised steps for carrying out a calculation or solving a problem.

Origin. Arabic name via medieval Latin. Abbasid-era mathematics · al-Khwarizmi · arithmetic and computing.

Earlier sense. Medieval European forms of al-Khwarizmi’s name came to identify methods of calculation using Hindu-Arabic numerals.

Current sense. A procedure defined clearly enough to be followed or implemented, often on a computer.

History and meaning

In the ninth century, scholars working in the Abbasid world drew on knowledge circulating among Arabic, Greek, Persian and Indian intellectual traditions. Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote across several mathematical subjects. One of his works concerned arithmetic using Indian numerals. The Arabic text of that arithmetic work has not survived in its original form, but material associated with it circulated in Latin versions. This matters because European readers could encounter both new ways to calculate and forms of a name that acquired a life of their own.

Medieval Latin forms of al-Khwarizmi’s name helped produce terms for a method of reckoning with Hindu-Arabic numerals. In English, algorism belongs to this history. A positional numeral system makes the place of a digit significant, and written operations can deal with columns of digits in a repeatable order. Over time, the modern spelling algorithm came to be associated with specified computational procedures more generally. A separate word, algebra, has a different connection to the same scholar: it comes through a term in the title of his work on equations, rather than through the Latin forms of his name.

Today, algorithms can sort a list, search a database or help rank information shown to a reader. The historical link with written arithmetic is useful because an algorithm can be explained before it is placed inside a computer. A description of what steps to follow differs from the programming language in which those steps are implemented. In public debate, people sometimes call any complex automated decision “the algorithm”. A more precise account identifies the inputs, the purpose, and the process or rules that connect incoming information to a result. That distinction makes it easier to ask who designed a system and what its results actually show.

Timeline

Early ninth century

Al-Khwarizmi writes on mathematics in the Abbasid world.

Twelfth century

Latin works transmit arithmetic associated with his name.

Medieval and early modern usage

Forms related to algorism refer to systematic computation.

Modern use

Algorithm refers broadly to organised procedures, especially computational ones.

Usage, pronunciation and eight examples

Common pairings. design an algorithm; a sorting algorithm; ranking algorithm; algorithmic bias

Related language. algorithms; algorithmic; algorithmically; related: procedure, formula, heuristic

Watch for this. An algorithm is a procedure. A software program implements procedures and may include several algorithms. An algorithm need not use artificial intelligence.

Say it aloud. Stress AL. Practise the voiced /ð/ sound as in this before the final weak syllable. Then read two examples, retaining the same word stress.

  1. The library uses an algorithm to suggest related titles.
  2. The programmer replaced the sorting algorithm with a faster one.
  3. A route-planning algorithm compares several possible journeys.
  4. The team tested the algorithm on records it had never seen before.
  5. The researcher described each step of the algorithm in plain English.
  6. Changing the inputs can change what an algorithm recommends.
  7. The committee asked whether the algorithm treated identical cases consistently.
  8. Two programs can implement the same algorithm in different languages.

The shops need a workable replacement and clear conditions for ending the boycott.

The survey and the headline

First listen for the main point. Then listen again for the evidence.

Read aloud by a UK voice supplied by your browser, when available.

Reveal transcript

Presenter Our first headline is “Council modernises heritage services”. The second is “Union warns jobs could be at risk”. Both refer to a fictional plan to relocate some services from Victoria Park. Sam, do the headlines contradict each other?

Sam They give different accounts of what matters most. The first repeats the council’s preferred description and makes the change sound settled. The second attributes a warning and keeps the risk conditional. The draft plan is what has been published. No outcome has been announced.

Presenter The residents’ group says a contractor was approached early. Is that evidence that the public consultation cannot make a difference?

Sam It is evidence that a group made an allegation. The council confirms a building survey, but a survey and a redevelopment contract are very different pieces of work. The records that matter are the survey specification, any contract and the list of decisions still open to change.

Presenter People might hear streamlining and picture job losses. Is that an unreasonable response?

Sam It is a possible inference. Combining services could lead to redundancies, especially if tasks overlap. It could also change where work happens without reducing posts. To evaluate that inference, ask for the staffing assumptions and compare them with what happened after similar reorganisations.

Presenter How would you write a neutral headline now?

Sam “Council consults on Victoria Park heritage services”. A subheading could mention the union’s concerns and the council’s position. That way the reader can distinguish the event from the possible consequences.

Listen for this. Which documents would help test the allegation that the decision was already made?

Reveal the answer

The survey specification, any procurement or contract record, and the scope of decisions open to consultation.

Speaking activity. Role-play a follow-up interview. Ask one question about a fact and one about an unresolved inference.

Council Pushes Forward

Read three fictional articles as an editor. Separate what can be reported directly, what should be attributed, what an audience might infer and what remains unresolved.

Fictional teaching scenario

Council pushes forward with heritage-services proposal

Victoria Park’s visitor centre and the council archives could move to a shared site under a draft plan released by the fictional Borough of Marlowbridge. The proposal lists two options for the park site: a long lease to a commercial operator or continued council management with reduced opening hours. A four-week consultation opened on Monday. The council says it is seeking views on both options and that a final vote is due in July.

Councillor Jenkins described the changes as a way to modernise facilities and make better use of the budget. The staff union says a merger could put jobs at risk and has asked for the staffing assumptions. A residents’ group says a contractor was approached before the consultation began. The council confirmed that it commissioned a survey of the buildings but said it had signed no redevelopment contract. The survey terms have not yet been made public.

If the council has commissioned a survey, that establishes that it gathered information. It does not, by itself, establish that redevelopment was approved. To investigate the residents’ claim, request the procurement notice, survey specification, any decision record and the documents setting out which outcomes the consultation can change.

Fictional teaching scenario

Last bus to Eastbank

The fictional town of Eastbank plans to run its last weekday bus at 10.30 pm rather than 11.15 pm during a three-month trial. The operator reports an average of nine passengers on the final departure and says it will add a trip at 6.15 pm, when buses are often crowded. A hospital staff group says some late-shift workers finish after 10.30 pm and cannot use the earlier bus.

One headline says “Operator adds peak-time service”. Another says “Hospital staff warn of lost late bus”. Both refer to the same proposal, but select different effects. A fuller account could report both changes, attribute the worker group’s concern and identify how the trial will measure journeys left unserved. The operator’s average does not show how many passengers have viable alternatives.

Fictional teaching scenario

Housing office merges contact desks

In the fictional city of Dalehaven, the housing office says it will combine its repairs and tenancy telephone lines next month. Officials describe the merger as streamlining and predict shorter waiting times. The council has published call volumes, but has not published staffing numbers or promised a target for waiting times. A residents’ association says the change could make complex cases harder to resolve.

The prediction and the residents’ concern remain claims about the future. A fair follow-up would compare answer times, calls abandoned and repeat contacts before and after the merger. A single average could conceal differences between routine repairs and complicated tenancy cases. A useful account would specify who made the prediction, how it will be tested and when the results will be available.

Newsroom vocabulary

Play the British pronunciation beside each transcription.

streamlining

/ˈstriːmlaɪnɪŋ/

Making a process more efficient or less complicated.

The council described the shared booking system as streamlining.

Check. Ask which steps will change, what will improve and whether any service will be removed.

consultation

/ˌkɒnsəlˈteɪʃən/

A process of seeking views before or during decision-making.

The consultation invited residents to comment on three proposals.

Check. Ask what is still open to change, whose views were sought and how responses will be reported.

restructuring

/ˌriːˈstrʌktʃərɪŋ/

Changing the way an organisation or activity is arranged.

The report proposed restructuring the library service across three sites.

Check. The word describes structural change. Check the plan before inferring closures or job losses.

redundancy

/rɪˈdʌndənsi/

In British employment use, loss of a job because the employer no longer needs the role.

The union requested figures for possible redundancies.

Check. A risk of redundancy and a confirmed redundancy are different claims.

backlash

/ˈbæklæʃ/

A strong adverse reaction to a change, decision or statement.

The proposed charges triggered a backlash among regular users.

Check. Check who objected and what the evidence says about the scale of opposition.

allegedly

/əˈledʒɪdli/

Used to signal that something has been claimed and is not presented as established.

The invoices were allegedly approved before the committee met.

Check. Identify who made the claim. The adverb alone does not establish who said it or whether it is true.

accountability

/əˌkaʊntəˈbɪləti/

The obligation to explain decisions and accept scrutiny or consequences.

The group asked for clearer accountability for the spending decision.

Check. Identify the decision-maker, whom they answer to and what happens after review.

transparency

/trænˈspærənsi/

Openness about information, decisions and how they are made.

Publishing the evaluation criteria would improve transparency.

Check. Look for information that is usable as well as available.

rhetoric

/ˈretərɪk/

Language used to persuade, or the study of persuasive language.

The speech’s rhetoric emphasised shared responsibility.

Check. Rhetoric need not be empty or dishonest. Identify the appeal and assess the supporting evidence.

framing

/ˈfreɪmɪŋ/

Presenting an issue from a particular perspective through selection and emphasis.

The headlines differed in their framing of the same change.

Check. Compare what each version foregrounds, omits and attributes.

spin

/spɪn/

An interpretation tailored to create a favourable impression.

The columnist called the description of cuts as savings political spin.

Check. Treat spin as an interpretation and justify it by comparing the wording with the facts.

bias

/ˈbaɪəs/

A tendency that systematically favours one perspective, group or result.

The study examined possible bias in the selection of interviewees.

Check. One strongly worded headline may suggest a perspective. A pattern across coverage can support a stronger claim.

austerity

/ɒˈsterəti/

Policies intended to restrain spending or reduce a public deficit, often through cuts or tax increases.

The report links shorter opening hours to austerity measures.

Check. Identify the policy and the decision-makers before suggesting why a specific service changed.

euphemism

/ˈjuːfəmɪzəm/

A milder or less direct expression used in place of one considered harsh, upsetting or embarrassing.

Campaigners questioned whether “service adjustment” was a euphemism for a cut.

Check. Identify the specific change before deciding what the phrase softens.

News-literacy check · 8 questions
1. News

What does the council confirm in the Victoria Park article?

The council confirms the survey and says no redevelopment contract was signed. The survey’s terms remain unavailable in the scenario.

2. News

Which headline preserves both timing and attribution?

The warning is attributed, and could retains the distinction between a risk and an event.

3. News

Which evidence would best help judge whether the consultation can influence the result?

A timetable and decision scope are stronger evidence than impressions of the language.

4. News

Which claim is an inference from the Eastbank proposal?

The timetable changes are stated. Their effect on particular passengers is a reasonable possibility that needs supporting evidence.

5. News

What does reportedly or allegedly tell a reader?

Such wording signals distance from a claim. Readers still need attribution and evidence.

6. News

How could Dalehaven measure whether the merger helps callers?

Multiple measures can test several kinds of effect and reveal problems an average might conceal.

7. News

Which reporting verb introduces a testable forecast without treating it as fact?

Predicted attributes a forward-looking claim rather than confirming an outcome.

8. News

How should a journalist handle the word backlash?

Backlash suggests a strong reaction. Attribution and evidence help establish its breadth and intensity.
