The test format

Four parts, forty questions, about thirty minutes

The Listening test is the same for Academic and General Training. The parts get harder as the test goes on, and you hear each recording once.

Part 1

Everyday conversation

Two people talk about an everyday task, such as booking a room, joining a club or reporting a lost bag.

Questions
1–10
Usually
form, note or table completion
Listen for
names spelt out, numbers, dates, prices and corrections
Part 2

Everyday monologue

One speaker gives information to the public: a tour, a talk to new members, a radio announcement.

Questions
11–20
Usually
multiple choice, map or plan labelling, matching
Listen for
directions, changes to plans and what is new or different
Part 3

Educational discussion

Two to four people, often students and a tutor, discuss an assignment, a project or a piece of research.

Questions
21–30
Usually
multiple choice, matching, sentence completion
Listen for
opinions, agreement and disagreement, and who decides what
Part 4

Academic lecture

One speaker gives a lecture or talk on an academic subject. There is no break in the middle of this part.

Questions
31–40
Usually
note, summary, sentence or flow-chart completion
Listen for
signposts such as firstly, however and so, to sum up
Reading time

Before each set of questions you get silent time (about 30 seconds) to read ahead. A countdown in the test shows how long is left and which questions to look at.

Recording

The recording plays once. In Part 1 an example is played first. Answers come in the same order as the questions in most question types.

Checking time

After each part you get half a minute to check. At the end of the test there are two minutes to check all your answers, as in the computer-delivered test. (On paper you would get ten minutes to copy answers to the answer sheet.)

Strategy guides

Every question type, and how to beat it

Open a guide to see what the task looks like, a step-by-step approach and the trap most candidates fall into. Each guide shows which tests have the most practice for it.

Form, note and table completionFacts in a structure: names, numbers, dates, places

What you see

A form, a set of notes or a table with gaps. The headings show how the information is organised and the order it will come in.

How to approach it

  1. Read the headings and the words around each gap, and decide what kind of word is missing: a name, a number, a plural noun, an adjective.
  2. Check the word limit. ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER allows harbour, 32 or 32 harbour.
  3. When a name is spelt out, write the letters as you hear them. Know how English speakers say double s and the letters A, E, I, G, J.
  4. Write only the missing words. Do not repeat words already printed beside the gap, such as £ or Room.
The trap

Speakers correct themselves. In “The meeting’s on the fifth… no, sorry, the sixth”, only the corrected date is right. Always wait for the end of the sentence.

Multiple choiceOne answer from three options

What you see

A question or an unfinished sentence and three options. Usually all three options are mentioned in some way, but only one answers the question.

How to approach it

  1. Underline the key idea in the question, not in the options. Then you know exactly what to listen for.
  2. Expect paraphrase: the recording will rarely use the words in the correct option.
  3. Listen for words that turn the meaning: but, actually, in fact, not at all, that wasn’t the idea.
  4. Cross out each option once it is clearly rejected, and choose only when the speaker finishes the point.
The trap

The first option you hear is often a distractor: “A lot of people think the café closed because of the rent, but the real reason was the new road.”

Multiple choice with more than one answerChoose TWO (or THREE) letters from a list

What you see

A question and five or more options. You choose a fixed number of letters. Each correct letter scores one mark, in any order.

How to approach it

  1. Read all the options first and group similar ones. Most will be mentioned, and several will be rejected.
  2. Tick possible answers lightly as you listen, then cross out the ones the speakers reject.
  3. Never choose more letters than the question asks for: extra letters do not earn extra marks.
The trap

An option is mentioned with enthusiasm and then ruled out: “A boat trip would be lovely, but they’re fully booked until May, so that’s out.”

MatchingMatch items to a list of people, opinions or features

What you see

A numbered list of items and a box of options marked A, B, C and so on. Some options may be used more than once, and some not at all.

How to approach it

  1. The numbered items come in order. The options in the box do not, so keep the box in view.
  2. Learn the options well during reading time: they are short, and they will be paraphrased.
  3. When two speakers talk, note who makes each decision. “I’ll take that section” and “Leave it to me” both signal a decision.
The trap

One speaker offers and the other declines, or both agree to share. Wait to hear the final decision before you choose.

Map and plan labellingPut places on a map, a floor plan or a site plan

What you see

A map or plan with letters on it, and a list of places to locate. The speaker usually walks you through the map in the order of the questions.

How to approach it

  1. Use the reading time to find the starting point (You are here, the entrance), north, and the main paths or corridors.
  2. Know the language of position: opposite, beyond, at the far end, on your right-hand side, just past, in the corner.
  3. Follow the route with your finger or pencil as the speaker moves.
  4. If the entrance is not at the bottom of the map, turn the map in your head: walking in from the top means your left is the map’s right.

Harder from Test 7. From Test 7 onwards the maps and plans have more letters than places, entrances on the side or top, routes that change direction, and speakers who correct themselves.

The trap

A nearby place is named first to confuse you: “You’ll pass the gift shop on your left. The toilets aren’t there; they’re the next door along.”

Sentence and summary completionComplete a sentence or a paragraph that paraphrases the recording

What you see

Sentences or a short summary with gaps. The surrounding words are a paraphrase, but the word you write is the word you hear.

How to approach it

  1. Read the sentence and predict the grammar of the gap: does it need a noun, a verb, a plural?
  2. Listen for the meaning of the sentence, not the same words. The answer usually comes just after a synonym of the words before the gap.
  3. Read your completed sentence again: it must be grammatical and make sense.
The trap

Singular and plural. If the sentence says “several ___”, a singular noun will be marked wrong.

Flow-chart and diagram completionStages of a process, or the parts of an object

What you see

A flow-chart of a process, or a diagram of a machine or structure with labels missing.

How to approach it

  1. Follow the arrows during reading time and work out the stages before you listen.
  2. Listen for sequence words: first, once that is done, at this stage, finally.
  3. For diagrams, look for position words: on top, underneath, attached to, inside.
The trap

A stage is described and then reversed: “people often think you heat it first, but in fact…”. Write the stage the speaker confirms.

Short-answer questionsAnswer a question in a few words

What you see

Direct questions, often starting with What, Where, Which or How many, with a word limit.

How to approach it

  1. Underline the question word: it tells you what kind of answer you need.
  2. Write words from the recording, within the limit. Articles such as a and the count as words.
  3. If a question asks for two things, you may hear three: listen for the one that is rejected.
The trap

Writing a full phrase. If the limit is TWO WORDS, “the old library” is three words and scores nothing.

Answer rules

How answers are marked

These tests are marked the way the real test is marked: the right word, spelt correctly, within the word limit.

Spelling counts

A misspelt answer scores nothing. British and American spellings are both accepted: colour or color, centre or center.

Keep to the word limit

An answer longer than the limit is wrong, even if it contains the right word. a, an and the count as words.

Numbers

Write 65 or sixty-five; both are accepted, but digits are faster and safer. A number written in digits counts as a number, not a word.

Dates and times

14 June, June 14 and 14/6 are all accepted, as are 11.15, 11:15 and 11.15 pm.

Plurals

If the speaker says allergies, check whether the gap needs a plural. The analysis shows you how often singular and plural slips cost you marks.

Capitals and symbols

Capital letters do not matter. Leave out symbols and words that are already printed next to the gap, such as £ or km.
