ConditionalsMeaning, Form, Choice & Control

A conditional sentence sets one thing against another: put this in place, and that follows.

A conditional sentence links a condition — normally a clause with if — to a result. The verb forms you choose do more than mark time: they tell your listener whether you see the condition as an everyday fact, a live possibility, a remote idea, or something that never happened at all. This laboratory works through the whole system, from the four classroom types to inversion, ellipsis, unless, if only and the forms that occur only in speech.

Before the four types

Two ways of cutting the same cake

Grammar books analyse conditionals as real and unreal. Coursebooks, teachers and students usually prefer zero, first, second and third (also called Type 0, 1, 2 and 3). These are two different ways of dividing up the same material, and this page uses both: the four types give you a sequence to learn, the real/unreal distinction explains everything the four types leave out.

REAL CONDITIONALS

The tenses behave normally

Real conditionals use exactly the verb tenses you would expect for the time they refer to. Nothing special is signalled; the speaker treats the situation as an ordinary part of the world.

If the children are playing in the garden, tell them to come in.

If you are going to fly to Paris, you'll need a new passport.

If you came late, what did you expect?Past time, past tense — this is a real conditional about the past, not a second conditional.

UNREAL CONDITIONALS

The tenses are shifted back on purpose

Unreal conditionals imagine impossible or unlikely futures, or alternative possibilities for the past. They use special verb forms whose job is to mark the idea as unreal.

Past simple = unlikely or impossible present or future.
If humans lived on Mars, do you think they'd get bored with the colour red?Lived is a past form, but the sentence is about now or the future. The past form signals how remote the speaker thinks the idea is.

Past perfect = an alternative past.
If Kennedy had survived, the US would be very different now.The past perfect signals that the event never happened.

Teacher insight FROM THE SOURCE

The source unit is explicit that neither classification is "the truth": they are two ways of looking at the same cake. The real/unreal analysis is arguably more accurate and covers the wide variety of conditionals used in the real world; the numbered types are favoured by many teachers because they allow a more sequenced way of presenting and practising the commonest patterns. Decide which suits your learners — and expect to need both eventually.

Clause order is free

The if clause may come first or second with no change of meaning: The machine works better if you change the oil regularly. / If prices are too high, demand is choked off.

The comma rule

When the if clause comes first, use a comma: If I see him, I'll pass on your message. When the main clause comes first, no comma is needed: I'll pass on your message if I see him.

Two clauses, one meaning

The if clause carries the condition; the main clause carries the result. Neither clause is a complete message on its own — except with if only, which often stands alone.

Beyond the core four

Advanced structures

Real English uses far more conditional machinery than four patterns. Everything below is standard, current English; the level badges show how much control a learner is likely to need, not who is allowed to look. B1 CORE B2 EXTENSION C1 CONTROL

Time relationships that cross

Mixed time reference: a past condition with a present result B2
If+past perfect →would / could / might+base form

The condition is an alternative past (past perfect, exactly as in the third conditional); the result is a present or general consequence, so the main clause has no have. This is not an exception to the system — it follows directly from the real/unreal analysis: the past perfect marks an unreal past, and would + base form marks an unreal present.

If I hadn't missed that train, I'd be at the conference now.I did miss it, so I'm not there.

If she had studied accountancy, she wouldn't be looking for work today.

If they hadn't cut the maintenance budget, the machine would still work.

Mixed time reference: a present condition with a past result C1
If+past simple →would / could / might+have + past participle

Here the condition is a permanent or present state seen as unreal, and the result is an alternative past.

If I spoke Mandarin, I would have taken that posting in Shanghai.I don't speak it — a lasting fact — so I didn't take the job.

If he weren't so cautious, he would have invested years ago.

Mixed tenses: past simple where a past perfect would be expected C1 FROM THE SOURCE

Sometimes a simple past tense is used with if where a past perfect would be normal. This is more common in American English.

If I knew you were coming, I'd have baked a cake.

If I had the money with me, I would have bought you one.

If I didn't have my walking boots on, I think I would have really hurt my foot.

Remember

Recognise this when you hear it; in careful writing and in examinations, use the past perfect (If I had known you were coming …).

Wishes and regrets

If only — three tense patterns B2

If only …! says that we would like things to be different. It means the same as I wish … but is more emphatic, and the clause often stands alone with no main clause at all.

Past form → present meaning
If only I knew more people!
If only I was better-looking!Were can be used instead of was: If only your father were here!

would + infinitive → future meaning
If only it would stop raining, we could go out.
If only somebody would smile!

Past perfect → past meaning
If only she hadn't told the police, everything would have been all right.
If only I'd heard in time, I could have helped.

Key idea

If only and I wish both say how we wish a past situation had been different, and both carry a strong element of regret. If only is the more emotional of the two.

Conditions without if

unless — "except if" B1

Unless has a similar meaning to if … not, in the sense of except if.

Come tomorrow unless I phone.= if I don't phone / except if I phone

I'll take the job unless the pay is too low.

I'm going to dig the garden this afternoon, unless it rains.

Let's have dinner out — unless you're too tired.

When unless cannot be used

Unless means "except if"; it is not used for other meanings of if … not. Compare:

✓We'll meet at 7.00 — unless my train's late. (= except if my train is late)
✗Unless my train's late it will be the first time this week. → If my train isn't late, it will be the first time this week.

✓I'll drive over and see you, unless the car breaks down.
✗I'll be surprised unless the car breaks down soon. → I'll be surprised if the car doesn't break down soon.

Test: if you can replace the clause with "except if" and keep the meaning, unless works. If the sentence is about a consequence of something not happening rather than an exception to a plan, use if … not.

Other words that make conditions: providing, provided, as long as, on condition that, suppose, imagine B2

Many words and expressions work in similar ways to if, and often with similar structures. Imagine (that), suppose (that) and supposing (that) talk about what might happen; providing (that), provided (that), as/so long as and on condition (that) make conditions.

Imagine we could all fly. Wouldn't that be fun!

Supposing you'd missed the train. What would you have done?

You can borrow my bike providing/provided you bring it back.

I'll give you the day off on condition that you work on Saturday morning.

You're welcome to stay with us as/so long as you share the expenses.

Key idea

The conditioning words are not interchangeable in tone. Provided and on condition that are firm and often contractual; as long as is neutral and conversational; supposing invites the listener to imagine, so it is common in speculative talk.

Leaving out if: conversational conditions C1

If is sometimes left out at the beginning of a sentence in a conversational style, especially when the speaker is making conditions or threats.

You want to get in, you pay like everybody else.= If you want to get in …

You touch me again, I'll kick your teeth in.

Leaving out if: formal inversion — Had I realised … C1

In formal and literary styles, if can be dropped and an auxiliary verb put before the subject. This happens mostly with were, had and should.

Were/Had/Should +subject+…

Were she my daughter, …= If she were my daughter …

Had I realised what you intended, …= If I had realised …

Should you change your mind, …= If you should change your mind …

Common error

Negatives are not contracted in this structure.
✓Had we not missed the plane, we would all have been killed in the crash.
✗Hadn't we missed the plane, …

Fine control of if itself

if … will: results, present truth, indirect questions, polite requests, insistence C1

We normally use a present tense with if to refer to the future (I'll phone you if I have time, NOT if I will have time). But in certain situations if … will is correct.

1 Results. Will after if can mean "if this will be the later result".
I'll give you £100 if I win the lottery. Winning is the condition — it must happen first.
I'll give you £100 if it'll help you to get home.Getting home is the result — it follows the gift of money.

2 "If it is true now that …"
If Anna won't be here on Thursday, we'd better cancel the meeting.
If prices will really come down in a few months, I'm not going to buy one now.

3 Indirect questions.
I don't know if I'll be ready in time. NOT if I'm ready in time.

4 Polite requests, where will means "are willing to".
If you will come this way, I'll show you your room.
If you would come this way … Would is more polite still.

5 Insistence, with stressed WILL, to criticise habits or choices.
If you WILL eat so much, it's not surprising you feel ill.= If you insist on eating so much …

if … happen to and if … was/were to B2

We can suggest that something is unlikely, or not particularly probable, with … happen to … after if.

If I happen to run into Daniel, I'll tell him to call you.

If you happen to pass a supermarket, perhaps you could pick up some eggs.Should is possible in British English with a similar meaning, but this is now unusual: If I should run into Daniel …

If … was/were to is another way of talking about unreal or imaginary future events, and can make a suggestion sound less direct.

If the boss was/were to come in now, we'd be in real trouble. = If the boss came in …

What would we do if I was/were to lose my job?

If you were to move your chair a bit, we could all sit down.

Restriction

This structure is not normally used with verbs like be or know, which refer to continuing situations: ✓If I knew her name … ✗If I were to know her name …

if it was/were not for and but for C1

This structure says that one event or situation changes everything.

If it wasn't/weren't for his wife's money he'd never be a director.= Without his wife's money, …

If it wasn't/weren't for the children, we could go skiing next week.

For the past: If it hadn't been for your help, I don't know what I'd have done.

But for your help, I don't know what I'd have done.But for means "if it were not for" or "if it had not been for", and is followed by a noun phrase.

Leaving out words after if; if so and if not B2

We sometimes leave out subject + be after if. Note the common fixed expressions if necessary, if any, if anything, if ever, if in doubt.

I'll work late tonight if necessary. = … if it is necessary

There is little if any good evidence for flying saucers.

I'm not angry. If anything, I feel a little surprised.

He seldom if ever travels abroad.

If in doubt, ask for help. = If you are in doubt …

If about to go on a long journey, try to have a good night's sleep.

After if, we can use so and not instead of repeating a whole clause.

Are you free? If so, let's go out for a meal.

I might see you tomorrow. If not, then it'll be Saturday.

Meanings of if that are not conditions C1

Extra negative after doubt. An extra not is sometimes put into if-clauses after expressions suggesting doubt or uncertainty.
I wonder if we shouldn't ask the doctor to look at Emily. = I wonder if we should ask …
I wouldn't be surprised if she didn't get married soon. = … if she got married soon.

if … then, to emphasise that one thing depends on another.
If she can't come to us, then we'll have to go and see her.

if meaning "even if".
I'll finish this job if it takes all night.
I wouldn't marry you if you were the last man in the world.

Admitting facts with if. An if-clause can admit a fact while giving a reason for it.
If I'm a bit sleepy, it's because I was up all night.

if meaning "I'm saying this in case".
There's some steak in the fridge if you're hungry.
If you want to go home, Anne's got your car keys.The steak exists whether you are hungry or not; the if-clause explains why the remark is being made.

if meaning "although", in a formal style, commonly with if + adjective and no verb.
His style, if simple, is pleasant to read.
The profits, if a little lower than last year's, are still extremely healthy.If is not as definite as although: it suggests a matter of opinion, or something not very important. The same idea can be expressed with may … but: His style may be simple, but it is pleasant to read.

Conditionals in speech

Spoken structures that are incorrect in formal writing C1

These forms occur frequently in natural speech. They are described here so that learners recognise them; they are not usually written, and are incorrect in formal writing.

would in both clauses. Very informal, quite common in speech.
It would be good if we'd get some rain.
How would we feel if this would happen to our family?

'd have … 'd have. If-clauses referring to the past are sometimes constructed with 'd have.
If I'd have known, I'd have told you.
It would have been funny if she'd have recognised him.
You wonder what the attitudes of both players would have been if Nadal would have gone on and won that second set. — from a tennis commentary

had've and would've. Full forms are sometimes used instead of the contracted 'd, for emphasis or in negatives. These are genuine examples from conversation.
I didn't know. But if I had've known …
We would never have met if he hadn't have crashed into my car.
If I would've had a gun, somebody might have got hurt.

Common error

Confusion about this structure sometimes leads people to write would of instead of would have: the two are pronounced the same, /ˈwʊdəv/, in normal speech. ✗If I'd known, I would of told you.

How conditionals sound

Saying conditionals

Conditional sentences are long, and English compresses everything that carries no new information. Most learner sentences are grammatically accurate but sound laboured because the auxiliaries are given full value.

Contractions carry the pattern

In speech will becomes 'll, would and had both become 'd, and have in would have reduces to /əv/. I'd have prepared for the meeting is normal; the full form sounds emphatic or contrastive.

One 'd, two words

If I'd known … contains had; I'd tell you contains would. Listeners work out which one from the clause and the verb form that follows: 'd + past participle is had, 'd + base form is would.

would have = /ˈwʊdəv/

The source notes that would have and would of are pronounced the same in normal speech — which is exactly why would of appears in careless writing. Hear the reduction, but never write it.

Stress carries the criticism

Normally will after if is avoided; in the insistence pattern it is not only present but stressed: If you WILL eat so much, it's not surprising you feel ill. The stress is the meaning — remove it and the sentence stops being a criticism.

The comma you can hear

When the if-clause comes first, speakers usually let the voice rise at the end of it and pause briefly before the result. That audible break is what the comma represents in writing. ADDITIONAL RECOMMENDATION

Prominence falls on new information

If itself is weak and unstressed; the words that matter are the ones naming the condition and the result. Drilling whole chains — the technique the source uses for all three main types — trains the rhythm of the pattern rather than the words of one sentence.

Two complete lesson plans

Both plans use the presentation and practice ideas in the source unit — chain stories, matched beginnings and endings, concept questions, regret situations — but organise them into two different methodologies. Timings assume a 90-minute class and can be cut to 60 by removing the marked stages.

The Consequence Exchange

A four-round game in which teams build, defend and trade chains of consequences. Points are won for accuracy and for plausibility, so students cannot succeed by producing correct forms about nonsense — the meaning of each conditional has to survive challenge from another team.

LEVELB1–B2, C1 extension
DURATION90 minutes
GRAMMAR FOCUSZero, first, second, third
GROUPING4 teams of 3–4
MATERIALS3 card decks, score grid

Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, learners will be better able to:

  • form zero, first, second and third conditionals accurately in both clause orders;
  • choose between first and second conditional according to how likely they judge a condition to be;
  • build extended chains in which the result of one sentence becomes the condition of the next;
  • challenge and repair other speakers' conditional sentences using grammatical reasons;
  • express regret about an unchangeable past using third conditional and if only.

Language outcomes

If + present simple → will/might/could + base form; If + past simple → would/could + base form; If + past perfect → would/could/might have + past participle; If only + past perfect; contracted 'll, 'd, would've; comma with a fronted if-clause.

Materials and preparation

  • Deck A — Laws (zero): 12 beginning cards and 12 ending cards of scientific, mechanical and institutional truths, printed on separate sets (If you heat water to 100°C / it boils; If invalid information is entered / an error message is displayed). Deliberately include some endings that belong to other beginnings.
  • Deck B — Openings: 16 situation cards (Magda thinks she will lose her job; Pierre is waiting for his exam results; a candidate with few qualifications waits outside an interview room; a man watches his boat leave the dock without him).
  • Deck C — Constraints: 12 cards naming a required structure (use a modal other than will, put the result clause first, use if only, use unless).
  • Score grid on the board; one timer; a "Challenge" token per team (see mechanics).
  • Preparation time: 20 minutes to print and cut decks; they are reusable.

Staged procedure

TimeStage & teacher instructionsStudent instructions / interaction
0–8 minRound 0 — Laws. Place about half of Deck A on the board, beginnings and endings mixed up. Ask the class to propose pairs so that the meaning is plausible; check the structure each time. Then add the remaining cards, some beginnings and some endings from different sentences, and have students work out the missing halves.Whole class, shouted suggestions, then pairs. "Match a beginning with an ending so the sentence is true and the grammar works."
8–14 minConcept checking. Take two matched sentences and run concept questions: If you spend all your money, you can't pay your rent. Is it possible you could spend all your money? (Yes) If you do, can you also pay your rent? (No) How definite is this? (100%)Whole class. Students answer chorally, then one student repeats the sentence with natural rhythm.
14–34 minRound 1 — Chain building (first conditional). Teams draw an Opening card and build a chain of five links, each link taking the result of the previous sentence as its new condition. Model the first two links on the board, then time the round: 8 minutes to build, 8 minutes to perform.Teams of 3–4. Roles: Chair (keeps the chain moving), Scribe (writes), Challenger (holds the token), Speaker (performs without notes).
34–38 minChallenge mechanic. While a team performs, any other team may spend its Challenge token once per round: accuracy challenge ("the verb after if is wrong") or plausibility challenge ("that result couldn't follow from that condition"). The teacher adjudicates in under 20 seconds.Listening teams. A successful challenge wins 2 points and costs the performing team 2; a failed challenge costs the challenger 1.
38–56 minRound 2 — The remote market (second conditional). Teams draw a new Opening card describing an unlikely situation and produce five sentences about it. Add a Constraint card to each team, which must be satisfied by at least two sentences.Teams. Before performing, each team must state why the situation is unlikely rather than probable — this is the justification for using past forms.
56–74 minRound 3 — Auction of regrets (third conditional). Tell a short disaster narrative (the missed boat: the alarm clock that didn't ring, the missed train, the argument, the boat leaving). Teams then bid: how many accurate third conditional sentences about the story can they produce in four minutes? Highest bidder plays first; meeting the bid scores the bid, missing it scores zero, exceeding it scores the bid plus one.Teams confer for 60 seconds before bidding. The bidding forces a realistic self-assessment of accuracy under time pressure.
74–84 minRound 4 — The impossible bank C1. Display four of the sentences students produced. Teams rewrite them without if: inversion (Had the alarm rung …), unless, but for, if only. Double points, no challenge tokens.Teams write on mini-whiteboards or paper and reveal simultaneously.
84–90 minScoring, feedback and reflection. Total the grid, then run delayed error correction on 6–8 sentences collected during play. Close with the reflection question below.Whole class; individual written reflection in the last two minutes.

Scoring rules

  • Each accurate, plausible link in a chain: 1 point; a chain of five unbroken links: +3 bonus.
  • Successful challenge: +2 to the challenger, −2 to the performer. Failed challenge: −1 to the challenger.
  • Constraint satisfied: +2. Auction: bid met = bid, missed = 0, exceeded = bid + 1.
  • Round 4 rewrites: 4 points each, all or nothing — the structure must be complete and correctly punctuated.

Differentiation

B1 ADAPTATION

Give the Scribe a sentence-frame strip (If + present simple, … will + base form). Reduce chains to three links. Allow the Speaker to read. Use only Decks A and B; drop Constraint cards and Round 4.

B2 ADAPTATION

Run all four rounds as written. Require at least one modal other than will per chain and one reversed clause order per team. Challenges must name the problem, not just point at it.

C1 EXTENSION

Add a fifth round in which teams must argue that the same situation could be stated with either a first or a second conditional, and justify the change of view. Introduce if … was/were to and if it hadn't been for as premium-value structures worth six points.

Anticipated errors and the teacher's response

  • If it will rain, we'll cancel. → Point at the if-clause and say "future how?" Elicit the present form. Reserve the explanation of if … will for the C1 round.
  • If I would have known … → Note that it is heard in speech but is not accepted in the game, and show the written standard.
  • Zero/first confusion ("If you drop a glass, it will break" for a general truth) → Ask: every glass, every time, or this glass now?
  • Chains that drift into nonsense to win speed points → This is exactly what the plausibility challenge exists to punish; let another team catch it before you do.
  • Missing comma with a fronted if-clause in Round 4 → All-or-nothing scoring makes the point without a lecture.

Assessment and checking

Collect one written chain per team and mark it against three criteria: form (verb forms and punctuation), coherence (does each result become the next condition?), and likelihood (does the pattern match the team's stated view of the situation?). The Round 3 bid also gives you a usable measure of how accurate students believe they are — compare it with how accurate they actually were.

Reflection and homework

  • Reflection prompt: "Which round was hardest, and was the difficulty in the grammar or in the thinking?"
  • Homework: write the Round 3 story as a ten-line narrative, then add five sentences beginning If only …. Alternatively, complete section J and section M of the student worksheet.

Teacher insight FROM THE SOURCE

Rounds 0 and 1 are direct adaptations of the source unit's "Beginnings and endings" card activity and its chain story technique, which the source recommends for the first, second and third conditionals alike. The source's advice about the chain story is worth keeping: for the first conditional, the events in the chain should all seem likely or possible, because that is what makes the tense choice meaningful.

The Contingency Report

A task-based lesson built on a real decision problem. Groups receive a one-off grant with conditions attached, choose between three options, and produce a contingency report that must state what will happen if things go well, what would happen if they went badly, and what would have happened had an earlier decision been taken. Conditionals are not the topic of the lesson; they are what the task cannot be completed without.

LEVELB1–C1 mixed
DURATION90 minutes
TASK TYPEDecision + persuasion
OUTCOMESpoken report + written plan
GROUPINGGroups of 3–4, then panels

Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, learners will have:

  • used conditionals to weigh consequences and negotiate a group decision;
  • distinguished, in their own output, between conditions they treat as real and conditions they treat as remote;
  • set conditions on an agreement using unless, provided (that), as long as and on condition that;
  • evaluated an alternative past with third and mixed conditionals;
  • revised their own language after a focused analysis stage.

Materials

  • A one-page memo from "the Board" containing eight conditionals embedded naturally (used for pre-task input, not for analysis yet).
  • Three option cards with costs, risks and a short history of a similar decision taken two years ago that failed.
  • Report template: Recommendation · Conditions · Risks · What we would do if …
  • Board panel question strip for the listening groups.
TimeStageWhat happens
0–10 minPre-task: framingStudents read the Board memo and answer three comprehension questions about what the Board wants and what it is worried about. No grammar is taught. The teacher checks the two conditions attached to the grant and asks: what happens if we don't meet them?
10–18 minPre-task: primingIn pairs, students recall a decision their institution took that did not work. They tell each other what happened. The teacher listens and notes — without correcting — any conditionals attempted. This creates the learner-generated language that stage 5 will work on.
18–42 minTaskGroups read the three option cards and must reach a single recommendation. Rules: every option must be discussed in terms of its consequences; the group must agree at least two conditions to attach to its recommendation; the group must say what it would do if the grant were reduced by half. Teacher circulates as a resource only, and does not correct.
42–56 minPlanningGroups draft the report on the template and rehearse the spoken version. This is where accuracy becomes the group's own concern: they know they will be questioned. The teacher is now available for language help and gives it on request, briefly and individually.
56–70 minReportEach group presents to a panel formed from another group. The panel must ask at least three questions from the strip: What would you do if …?, What will happen if …?, Would you still recommend this if …? Panels record one sentence they thought was persuasive and one they did not understand.
70–82 minLanguage focusThe teacher puts up 8–10 sentences taken from what students actually said, some accurate and some not, unattributed. Students sort them: real or unreal? then correct the faulty ones. Only now does the teacher name the patterns and add the conditioning words the task required (provided, as long as, on condition that, unless), plus the Board memo's own examples for comparison.
82–90 minPost-task practiceGroups rewrite the three weakest sentences from their own report and read the improved version aloud. Quick repeat of the task in 90 seconds: "Persuade a different panel in three sentences."

Language focus in detail

The analysis stage is organised around three questions rather than three rules:

  • Is this condition real for the speaker? Sorting student sentences into real and unreal before naming any type keeps the focus on the decision, which is where the meaning lives.
  • Does the verb form match that judgement? A group that says If the grant was reduced … while believing a reduction is likely has a mismatch worth discussing; the source is explicit that this difference is a matter of individual view, not fact.
  • How firm is the condition? We'll do it if you fund it, provided you fund it, and on condition that you fund it commit the speaker to different degrees.

Differentiation

B1 ADAPTATION

Supply the template with the first three words of each section (If we choose …, We will only agree if …). Allow the fallback question to be answered with a first conditional. Pair a B1 student with a stronger writer for the planning stage.

B2 ADAPTATION

Require at least one unless and one provided/as long as in the written conditions, and one third conditional about the failed decision two years ago.

C1 EXTENSION

Add a hostile panel member who must challenge every claim, forcing mixed conditionals (If we had taken that route in 2023, we wouldn't be discussing this now) and inversion in the written report.

Teacher role and learner role

Teacher: designer and manager of the task; a silent resource during the task itself; a collector of data throughout; an analyst in the language focus stage; a corrector only after meaning has been achieved. The single hardest discipline in this lesson is not correcting during the task cycle.

Learner: decision-maker and negotiator first, language user second. Learners choose what to say; the report format and the panel questions create the need for conditionals without the teacher prescribing them. In the planning stage learners become their own editors.

Anticipated difficulties and responses

  • Groups decide too fast. Insert a rule: every option must be defended by someone before it is rejected.
  • Avoidance. Some learners will say maybe we cancel it rather than risk a conditional. The panel question strip forces the structure, because a bare maybe does not answer What would you do if …?
  • Overuse of a single pattern. If everything comes out as first conditional, ask the group to rate each condition from 0 to 100 and see whether the forms match the numbers.
  • Grammar anxiety in the planning stage. Limit help to the sentence a learner shows you, and return the pen to them.
  • Mixed-level groups. Assign roles by strength: chair, note-taker, spokesperson, timekeeper, and rotate for the repeat task.

Feedback strategy

Hot correction is reserved for breakdowns in communication during the report. Everything else is delayed and anonymous: sentences go on the board without names, students repair them in pairs, and only then does the teacher confirm. Finish by displaying two sentences that were strikingly effective and asking what made them work — praise is more useful when it is specific about language.

Extension and homework

  • Write the contingency report formally (200–250 words), using at least one inverted conditional and one unless clause.
  • Complete sections H and I of the student worksheet.
  • Optional: record a two-minute voice note answering "If the grant had arrived two years earlier, what would be different now?"

Teacher insight ADDITIONAL RECOMMENDATION

This plan is not taken from the source unit, which is organised around presentation and practice rather than task cycles. What it borrows is the source's insistence that the difference between first and second conditional is a matter of the speaker's view rather than fact: the "rate the condition from 0 to 100" move in the language focus stage exists to make that view visible and therefore discussable.
