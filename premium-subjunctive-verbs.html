Introduction to Subjunctive and Reporting Verbs

In advanced English grammar, certain verbs dictate specific structural patterns in the clauses that follow them. Verbs expressing necessity, advice, suggestion, or request often trigger the subjunctive mood (using the base form of the verb) or require specific gerund (-ing) or infinitive constructions.

Understanding the difference between advise, recommend, and suggest is critical for precise communication, especially in formal or academic contexts.

This guide provides an exhaustive breakdown of these verbs, their acceptable patterns, and comprehensive examples. Test your mastery with the self-check quizzes at the end of each section, or use the sidebar's Worksheet tab for a printable classroom assessment.

The Subjunctive Mood: Definition & Forms

The subjunctive mood expresses ideas that are not stated as objective fact — necessities, suggestions, demands, wishes, and hypothetical situations — as opposed to the indicative mood, which reports things as they actually are. Modern English keeps two distinct subjunctive forms:

1. The Mandative (Present) Subjunctive

Uses the base form of the verb — no -s, no tense marking, and be instead of is/am/are — inside a that-clause following a verb or adjective of necessity, recommendation, or demand. This is the form used throughout the Suggest, Recommend, and Request sections of this course.

  • The committee insists that he arrive on time. (not "arrives")
  • It is essential that she be present. (not "is")
  • The judge ordered that the evidence be released.

2. The Were-Subjunctive (Past/Hypothetical)

Uses were instead of was for all persons, in unreal, hypothetical, or counterfactual conditions and wishes. This is a different construction from the mandative subjunctive above and is not covered by the verb patterns in this course.

  • If I were you, I would accept the offer.
  • I wish it were Friday already.
  • She spoke as if she were in charge.

Common Mandative-Subjunctive Trigger Verbs

Beyond suggest, recommend, and request, a wide family of formal verbs and expressions trigger the same base-verb subjunctive pattern. Recognizing the whole family makes the rule much easier to internalize.

insist

She insisted that he pay for the damages immediately.

demand

The union demanded that management reconsider the offer.

require

The policy requires that every visitor sign in at reception.

propose

I propose that the vote be postponed until Monday.

urge

Officials urged that residents evacuate before the storm.

stipulate

The contract stipulates that payment be made within 30 days.

prefer

I would prefer that you call before visiting.

it is essential / vital / crucial

It is vital that the medication be refrigerated.

it is important / necessary

It is important that she know the full story.

Negating the Subjunctive

Not goes directly before the base verb — never with don't/doesn't, which shifts the sentence back into the indicative and changes the register.
The board insists that she doesn't attend. ➔ The board insists that she not attend.

British vs. American usage: American English typically uses the bare mandative subjunctive (recommended that he go), while British English often inserts should as an alternative (recommended that he should go) — both are fully correct. See the Suggest tab for a closer look at this pattern.
1

Suggest

The verb suggest is used to introduce an idea, plan, or action for consideration. It is a classic trigger for the subjunctive mood in formal English.

Common Error Alert

Never use an infinitive after suggest.
I suggest him to go. ➔ I suggest (that) he go.

Verb Patterns

1. suggest + (that) + subject + base verb

This is the subjunctive mood, typical in formal contexts. The 'that' is optional.

  • I suggest (that) she leave immediately.
  • The doctor suggested that he stop smoking.
  • It was suggested that a new committee be formed.

2. suggest + (that) + subject + should + base verb

Common in British English as an alternative to the pure subjunctive.

  • I suggest that she should leave immediately.
  • He suggested that we should meet earlier.

3. suggest + verb-ing (Gerund)

Used when the subject of 'suggest' is also involved in the action, or when the actor is general/unspecified.

  • I suggest taking the train to avoid traffic.
  • She suggested going to a movie.

4. suggest + noun phrase (+ to someone)

Used to propose a thing or a person.

  • Can you suggest a good restaurant?
  • I suggested this book to my students.
  • He suggested a new strategy to the board.

2

Recommend

Recommend is used to tell someone that something is good or useful, or to advise them to do something. It functions very similarly to 'suggest' but carries a slightly stronger sense of endorsement or professional advice.

Verb Patterns

1. recommend + (that) + clause (Subjunctive)

Often uses the base form of the verb, especially in American English.

  • The IT security team recommended (that) we change our passwords.
  • I strongly recommend that he not go there alone.

2. recommend + verb-ing (Gerund)

Used when speaking generally without specifying exactly who should perform the action.

  • I don't recommend spending more than two hours on social media.
  • Experts recommend drinking plenty of water.

3. recommend + noun + to + someone

Endorsing a specific thing or person to another party.

  • My sister recommended Instagram to me.
  • Can you recommend a good mechanic to us?

Difference from 'Advise'

Unlike 'suggest' and 'recommend', advise frequently takes the pattern: advise + object + infinitive.

  • Correct: They advised us to leave.
  • Incorrect: They recommended us to leave.

3

Request

Request is a formal way to ask for something. It is highly structured and often employed in official, business, or formal written correspondence. Like suggest, it frequently triggers the subjunctive mood.

Verb Patterns

1. request + (that) + clause (Subjunctive)

The most formal usage, demanding the base verb form.

  • The manager requested that everyone be on time.
  • They requested that he not leave the premises.

2. request + noun / noun phrase

Asking for a specific item or action directly.

  • We requested a table by the window.
  • The defendant requested an appeal.

3. request + someone + to + infinitive

Often used in the passive voice in formal signs or announcements.

  • Passengers are requested to remain seated.
  • I requested him to lower his voice. (Less common in active voice, 'asked' is preferred).
