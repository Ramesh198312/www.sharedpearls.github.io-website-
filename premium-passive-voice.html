The Passive
Voice

One clause, two ways of telling it. The passive moves the affected participant into subject position and lets the doer step back — or disappear entirely.

Section 1

What the passive voice actually does

Form first, then function — and the two are not the same thing.

The definition

A clause is passive when the grammatical subject is not the participant that performs the action, but the participant that undergoes or is affected by it. The verb phrase is built from a form of BE followed by the past participle of the main verb.

  • They built this house in 1486. active
  • This house was built in 1486. passive
  • Most Austrians speak German. active
  • German is spoken by most Austrians. passive
  • A friend of ours is repairing the roof. active
  • The roof is being repaired by a friend of ours. passive

When A does something to B there are often two ways of talking about it. If we want A — the doer, or agent — to be the subject, we use an active verb. If we want B, the receiver of the action, to be the subject, we use a passive verb.

The structural relationship

The rule that makes everything else work: the object of an active verb corresponds to the subject of a passive verb.

VoiceSubjectVerbObject / rest
activeTheybuiltthis house in 1486.
passiveThis housewas builtin 1486.
The boxed object of the active clause moves into subject position in the passive clause.

Where the doer goes

In most cases the subject of the active verb is simply not expressed in the corresponding passive sentence. If it does have to be expressed, this usually happens in a phrase with by, and the noun is called the agent.

  • This house was built in 1486 by Sir John Latton.

A number worth remembering. Agents are mentioned in only about twenty per cent of passive clauses. Leaving the agent out is the normal case, not an omission that needs excusing.

Why writers choose the passive

1. Interest is in the action, not the doer

We often choose a passive when we want to talk about an action but are not interested in saying — or are not able to say — who or what does it. Agentless passives are common in academic and scientific writing for exactly this reason.

  • Those pyramids were built around 400 AD.
  • Too many books have been written about the Second World War.
  • The results have not yet been analysed.

2. Putting the news at the end

English prefers to begin a sentence with something already known and to put the new information at the end. The passive is one of the main tools for arranging that.

  • 'What's Jack doing?' 'Jack's painting my portrait.'
  • 'Nice picture.' 'Yes, it was painted by my grandmother.'

3. Keeping the same subject

To go on talking about the same person or thing, it may be necessary to switch from active to passive and back.

  • He waited for two hours; then he was seen by a doctor; then he was sent back to the waiting room… then he was taken upstairs and examined by a specialist, after which he had to wait for another hour before he was allowed to go home.

This is far more natural than He waited for two hours; then a doctor saw him…, which changes subject in every clause.

4. Putting heavier expressions at the end

Long, heavy expressions tend to go at the end of a clause, and this too can be a reason for choosing a passive.

  • I was annoyed by Emily telling everybody what to do.
  • Emily telling everybody what to do annoyed me. heavy subject

Form and meaning are different questions

Meaning and grammar do not always go together. Not all active verbs have 'active' meanings: if you say that somebody receives something, or suffers, you are really saying that something is done to them.

Some English active verbs might be translated by passive or reflexive verbs in other languages, and some English passives might be translated by active or reflexive verbs.

  • My shoes are wearing out.
  • Suddenly the door opened.
  • I was born in 1956.
  • English is spoken here.

Some verbs work in both voices with similar meanings — to worry / to be worried, to drown / to be drowned. Sometimes active and passive infinitives are almost interchangeable: There's a lot of work to do / to be done. And some active verbs carry both active and passive meanings: She opened the door / The door opened.

Do not assume the passive is more formal or more academic by default. It is more formal only where it is doing a job — hiding an irrelevant agent, or arranging information. A passive used where the active would be clearer makes writing worse, not more scholarly. Section 6 of this module treats that choice directly.

Where the terminology becomes sharper

At B2 and C1 it is useful to separate three things that learners often merge:

  • Voice — a property of the verb phrase: BE + past participle.
  • Semantic role — who does what to whom. A passive subject is usually the patient or affected participant.
  • Discourse function — what the choice achieves in the text: topic continuity, end-focus, agent suppression, register.

A sentence can be passive in form while its discourse function is purely about arranging information, and it can be active in form while describing something that happens to its subject.

Section 2

Core grammar: making the transformation

Five decisions, in order. Get the order right and the form follows.

The procedure

  1. Find the object of the active clause. If there is no object, there is no ordinary passive. She arrived has nothing to move.
  2. Move that object into subject position. Pronouns change form: me → I, him → he, them → they.
  3. Choose the form of BE that matches the original tense. The tense is carried entirely by BE. The main verb never changes again.
  4. Put the main verb into the past participle. Regular verbs take -ed; irregular verbs must be known.
  5. Decide what happens to the original subject. Delete it, or express it with by if it carries information the reader needs.

Worked example — regular verb

  • The committee discussed the proposal yesterday.
  • The proposal was discussed yesterday.

Worked example — irregular verb

  • Someone has taken my umbrella.
  • My umbrella has been taken.

Worked example — multi-word verb

Verbs made up of more than one word can have passive forms if they are transitive.

  • The furniture was broken up for firewood.
  • She likes being looked at.
  • I need to be taken care of.
  • He hates being made a fool of.

by + agent, and the prepositions that are not by

In a passive clause we usually use by to introduce the agent — the person or thing that does the action, or that causes what happens.

  • All the trouble was caused by your mother.
  • These carpets are made by children who work twelve hours a day.

Participles that behave like adjectives

Some past participles are more like adjectives than verbs — shocked, worried, frightened. After these we often use other prepositions instead of by, and such structures are not really passives at all but examples of be + adjective.

  • I was shocked at/by your attitude.
  • We were worried about/by her silence.
  • Are you frightened of spiders?

Agent or instrument?

With is used when we talk about an instrument which is used by an agent to do an action. This is one of the most persistent B1–B2 errors.

  • He was shot by a rifle.
  • He was shot (by the policeman) with a rifle.

Why learners confuse active and passive forms

These mistakes are not carelessness. Two facts about English make them almost inevitable:

  1. BE appears in passive verb forms and in active progressive tenses.
  2. Past participles appear in passive verb forms and in active perfect tenses.

So the same two building blocks construct two completely different systems. Compare:

SentenceVoiceForm
He was calling.activepast progressive
He was called.passivesimple past
He has called.activepresent perfect
He has been called.passivepresent perfect passive

The classic misformations

  • I was very interesting in the lesson. → interested
  • We were questioning by the immigration officer. → were questioned
  • She has put in prison for life. → has been put
  • These houses build in wood. → are built of
  • The play performed in the evening. → was performed
  • This exhibition will be visit 5 million people. → will be visited by

Section 4

Modal passives

The one area where the passive almost never changes the meaning — and the neighbouring area where it changes everything.

The three patterns

PatternTime referenceExample
modal + be + pppresent / futureIt can be done tomorrow.
modal + have been + pppastThe defect should have been identified earlier.
semi-modal + be + ppvaries with the semi-modalEvery unit has to be replaced.

Modal structures can be passivised

  • We can do it tomorrow. active
  • It can be done tomorrow. passive
  • They may close the hospital.
  • The hospital may be closed.

Why the meaning survives

A modal verb's meaning usually 'spreads over' the whole clause rather than attaching to the subject. This means a modal structure can be changed from active to passive without affecting the meaning very much.

  • A child could understand his theory.
  • His theory could be understood by a child. same claim
  • You shouldn't put adverbs between the verb and the object.
  • Adverbs shouldn't be put between the verb and the object. same advice
  • Dogs may chase cats.
  • Cats may get chased by dogs. same possibility

And where it does not survive. With most other verbs that are followed by infinitives, the meaning is attached to the subject, so a change from active to passive changes the sense of the sentence completely.

  • Dogs like to chase cats.
  • Cats like to be chased by dogs. different — and untrue
  • Dan wants to phone Anna.
  • Anna wants to be phoned by Dan. not the same meaning

The full modal set in the passive

ModalPassive exampleNote on use

Grammatical possibility is not the same as natural usage: be able to in the passive is a construction to recognise rather than to write.

Negatives, questions and perfect forms

Negative

The negative attaches to the modal, never to be or to have been.

  • Photographs may not be taken here.
  • The thermostat might not have been tested properly.
  • The thermostat might have not been tested properly.

Interrogative

The modal moves in front of the subject; everything after it stays in order.

  • Can the device be repaired at home?
  • When must the product be sent back?
  • Should this have been reported earlier?

Modal perfect passives and meaning

The perfect form shifts the whole structure into the past, and with must it usually shifts the meaning from obligation to deduction.

FormMeaning
It must be replaced.obligation, now or in future
It must have been replaced.deduction about the past — I am almost certain it was
It should be checked.advice for now
It should have been checked.criticism — it was not, and it ought to have been

Common learner errors. must be return (base form instead of participle); must is replaced (inflected BE after a modal); must have been replaced by Friday (perfect form used for a future deadline).

Section 5

Advanced structures

Everything beyond the basic transformation: two objects, prepositions that stay put, reporting structures, infinitives, and the informal passive with get.

A. Verbs with two objects

Many verbs — give, send, show, lend, pay, promise, refuse, tell, offer — can be followed by two objects: an indirect object (usually a person) and a direct object (usually a thing). Two active structures are possible.

Structure A: verb + indirect object + direct object

  • She gave her sister the car.
  • I had already shown the police officer Sam's photo.

Structure B: verb + direct object + preposition + indirect object

  • She gave the car to her sister.
  • I had already shown Sam's photo to the police officer.

Both of these structures can be made passive, which gives two possible passive clauses for the same event.

A → indirect object becomes the subject

  • Her sister was given the car.
  • The police officer had already been shown Sam's photo.

B → direct object becomes the subject

  • The car was given to her sister.
  • Sam's photo had already been shown to the police officer.

The choice between the two may depend on what has been said before, or on what needs to be put last in the sentence. Structure A — the person as subject — is the more common of the two.

  • I've just been sent a whole lot of information.
  • You were lent ten thousand pounds last year.
  • The visitors were shown a collection of old manuscripts.
  • They are being paid a lot of money for doing very little.
  • He was refused a visa because he had been in prison.
  • We will never be told the real truth.
  • How much have you been offered?

Two details that catch learners out

In structure B, prepositions are sometimes dropped before indirect object pronouns: This watch was given (to) me by my father.

Explain and suggest cannot be used in structure A. This is not a stylistic preference; the sentences are simply not English.

  • The problem was explained to the children.
  • The children were explained the problem.
  • A meeting place was suggested to us.
  • We were suggested a meeting place.

A terminological caution: prepositional phrases such as to the police officer are not called 'indirect objects' in all grammars. If your coursebook analyses them differently, the sentences remain the same; only the label changes.

B. Verbs with prepositions

The objects of prepositional verbs can become subjects in passive structures. Note the word order carefully: the preposition cannot be dropped.

  • We have looked at the plan carefully. → passive
  • The plan has been looked at carefully.
  • Nobody listens to her. →
  • She is never listened to.
  • Somebody has paid for your meal. →
  • Your meal has been paid for.
  • I don't like to be shouted.
  • I don't like to be shouted at.

Phrasal-prepositional verbs behave in the same way, and so do idiomatic multi-word verbs, provided they are transitive: He hates being made a fool of; I need to be taken care of.

Why learners should not strip the preposition

The preposition is part of the verb's lexical identity. Look and look at are different verbs; listen and listen to are different verbs. Removing the preposition does not tidy the sentence, it changes or destroys the meaning.

The limit: a second object after a preposition

If there is already a direct object, the second object — the one after the preposition — cannot become a passive subject.

  • They threw stones at him. →
  • Stones were thrown at him.
  • He was thrown stones at.
  • They stole a bicycle from him. →
  • A bicycle was stolen from him.
  • He was stolen a bicycle from.
  • They poured water on us. →
  • Water was poured on us.
  • We were poured water on.

Possessives cannot become passive subjects either

  • They called Mr Archer's name. → Mr Archer's name was called.
  • Mr Archer was name called.
  • I broke her mirror. → Her mirror was broken.
  • She was broken her mirror.

C. Passive reporting structures

Clause objects and preparatory it

Some sentences have clauses as their objects. These cannot normally become the subjects of passive sentences.

  • That she was a spy was thought by nobody.
  • That he was the right man for the job was felt.
  • That his company is in trouble is said.

However, passive structures are perfectly possible with preparatory it. This is the impersonal passive.

  • It was thought that she was a spy.
  • It was felt that he was the right man for the job.
  • It is said that his company is in trouble.

Infinitive objects

A few verbs followed by infinitives — decide, agree — can also be used in passive structures beginning with it. Most verbs cannot.

  • They decided to meet at twelve. → It was decided to meet at twelve.
  • We agreed to open a new branch. → It was agreed to open a new branch.
  • It is expected to make a profit this year.
  • It is not expected to rain today.

The personal passive: He is believed to be…

Many verbs can be followed by object + infinitive, and in most cases these structures can be made passive.

  • I was asked to send a stamped addressed envelope.
  • Felicity was chosen to be the Carnival Queen.
  • We were told not to come back.
  • They are allowed to visit Harry once a week.

With verbs like think, feel, believe, know, the active object + infinitive structure is rather formal and often unusual — They believe him to be dangerous is less usual than They believe that he is… — but the passive structure is common and occurs frequently in news reports.

  • He is believed to be dangerous.
  • Moriarty is thought to be in Switzerland.
  • She is known to have been married before.
  • It is considered to be the finest cathedral in Scotland.

With say, the infinitive structure is only possible in the passive.

  • His company is said to be in trouble.
  • They say his company to be in trouble.

hear, see, make and help

These verbs are followed in active structures by object + infinitive without to. In passive structures, to-infinitives are used.

ActivePassive
I saw him come out of the house.He was seen to come out of the house.
They made him tell them everything.He was made to tell them everything.
They helped him (to) get out of the country.He was helped to get out of the country.

Preparatory there

With some verbs — say, think, feel, report, presume, understand — the passive structure is possible with there as a preparatory subject.

  • There are thought to be more than 3,000 different languages in the world. = It is thought that there are…
  • There was said to be disagreement between Ministers.

Perfect, progressive and passive infinitives

A passive verb can be followed by a perfect, progressive or passive infinitive. This is where C1 precision is decided: the infinitive carries the time relationship between the report and the event.

  • He is believed to have crossed the frontier last night. event before report
  • I was told to be waiting outside the station at six o'clock. progressive
  • The hostages are expected to be released today. passive infinitive
  • Staff are said to have been told nothing. perfect passive infinitive

Exception: wanting and liking. Verbs that refer to wanting, liking and similar ideas cannot usually be used in passive structures with infinitives after them.

  • Everybody wanted Doris to be the manager.
  • Doris was wanted to be the manager.
  • We like our staff to say what they think.
  • Our staff are liked to say what they think.

Impersonal or personal — which to choose

PatternEffectRegister
It is said that his company is in trouble.Foregrounds the claim itself; slightly more distant.Formal, institutional, academic
His company is said to be in trouble.Foregrounds the entity; compresses the sentence.News writing, formal report
People say his company is in trouble.Names a vague source; more conversational.Neutral to informal

D. Passive infinitives and passive -ing forms

Passive infinitives

  • (to) be taken · (to) have been invited
  • There's a lot of work to be done.
  • She expects to be promoted in the spring.
  • He was lucky to have been chosen at all.

Note that active and passive infinitives are sometimes used with very similar meanings: There's a lot of work to do / to be done.

Passive -ing forms

  • being watched · having been invited
  • She likes being looked at.
  • He hates being made a fool of.
  • Having been warned twice, he said nothing.

After need and want, an active -ing form can carry a passive meaning: My watch needs cleaning = needs to be cleaned.

Where these appear in formal writing

  • The samples are required to be stored below four degrees.
  • The policy appears to have been drafted without consultation.
  • Being classified as low-risk, the site was not inspected again.

E. get as a passive auxiliary

Get + past participle can be used to make passive structures in the same way as be + past participle. This structure is mostly used in an informal style. It is often used to talk about events that happen by accident, unexpectedly, or outside one's control, and that have good or bad consequences.

  • I get paid on Fridays.
  • She's always getting invited to parties.
  • My watch got broken while I was playing with the children.
  • He got caught by the police speeding through the town centre.

When be is required instead

The passive with be is preferred for longer, more deliberate, planned actions.

  • The Emperor Charlemagne was crowned in the year 800. not got crowned
  • The new school will be opened by the Prime Minister on May 25th.
  • Our house was built in 1827.
  • Our house got built in 1827.

Where get is out of place. Academic articles, legal documents, formal reports, public notices and institutional correspondence. A get-passive in a research paper reads as an error of register even though it breaks no rule of syntax.

F. Negatives and questions

Negative statements

The negative particle attaches to the first auxiliary in the verb phrase.

  • I wasn't invited, but I went.
  • The results have not yet been analysed.
  • The samples were not being stored correctly.
  • The results have not yet analysed.

Yes/no questions

  • Has Emily been told?
  • Was the letter sent yesterday?
  • Is the house being painted at the moment?

Wh-questions

  • When was this cathedral built?
  • How much have you been offered?
  • Who was the parcel sent to?
  • What will have been decided by Friday?

Word-order errors to watch

  • When did this cathedral built?
  • The painting was acquired when?
  • Was sent the letter yesterday?

G. Imperatives and intransitive verbs

Passive imperatives

English has a passive imperative — Be seated; Be warned; Don't be fooled — but its range is narrow and it sounds formal or literary. For instructions, English normally uses an active imperative or a modal passive instead.

  • Be seated, please. formal, fixed
  • Don't be alarmed by the noise.
  • Natural alternatives: Please return the unit. / The unit must be returned.
  • Be returned the unit to the retailer.

Get also produces imperatives — Get vaccinated; Don't get caught — which are informal but perfectly current.

Intransitive verbs cannot form ordinary passives

Passive structures are impossible with intransitive verbs such as die or arrive, which cannot have objects, because there is nothing to become the subject of a passive sentence.

  • He was arrived at six o'clock.
  • She was died last year.
  • The accident was happened on the motorway.

Three things learners mistake for passives

  • Ergative verbs. The door opened; the glass broke; the shop closes at six. Active in form, and the subject is affected — but there is no be + participle and no implied agent.
  • Adjectival participles. I was interested; we are worried; he is retired. These are be + adjective, which is why other prepositions follow them.
  • Middle constructions. This book sells well; the meat cuts easily. Active in form, generic in meaning, and impossible in the passive.

H. Verbs with limited or special passive patterns

Stative verbs

Not all verbs can have passive forms. Some transitive verbs are seldom used in the passive; most of these are stative verbs, which refer to states rather than actions.

VerbActive — correctPassive — not English

Object complements: He was considered a genius

After some verbs the direct object can be followed by an object complement — a noun or adjective which describes or classifies the object. In passive clauses these become subject complements and come after the verb.

ActivePassive
Queen Victoria considered him a genius.He was considered a genius by Queen Victoria.
They elected Mrs Sanderson President.Mrs Sanderson was elected President.
We all regarded Kathy as an expert.Kathy was regarded as an expert.
Most people saw him as a sort of clown.He was seen as a sort of clown.
The other children called her stupid.She was called stupid by the other children.

Action or resulting state: My suitcase is packed

Some verbs refer to actions that produce a finished result — cut, build, pack, close. Others do not — push, live, speak, hit, carry. The past participles of finished-result verbs, and some of their passive tenses, can have two meanings: they can refer to the action, or they can describe the result, rather like adjectives.

  • The theatre was closed by the police on the orders of the mayor. the action
  • When I got there I found that the theatre was closed. the state

Because of this, be + past participle can have a meaning similar to a present perfect passive.

  • The vegetables are all cut up — what shall I do now? = have all been cut up
  • I got caught in the rain and my suit's ruined. = has been ruined
  • I think your ankle is broken. = has been broken
  • My suitcase is packed. = has been packed

Section 6

The passive at B2 and C1: register, discourse and judgement

At this level the question is no longer can I form it? but should I use it here?

Academic and scientific writing

Agentless passives are common in academic and scientific writing because the interest is in the procedure and the finding, not in the researcher. The convention is real, but it is weaker than it used to be: many journals — particularly in the sciences — now actively encourage the first person for describing what the authors did.

  • The samples were heated to 180°C for twenty minutes.
  • Participants were recruited through university mailing lists.
  • The results have not yet been analysed.
  • Three variables were excluded from the final model.

A working principle

Use the passive for method, where the agent is the standard researcher and therefore uninformative. Consider the active for argument, where the writer is making a claim and should own it. We argue that… is clearer, and more honest, than It is argued that…

British and American practice. Both traditions use the methodological passive. American style guides — and much North American journal guidance — push harder towards the active voice and the first person than their British counterparts, particularly in the social sciences. Follow the style guide of the journal you are writing for; neither preference is a rule of grammar.

News, institutional and legal writing

In news writing, passive reporting structures let a publication carry a claim without asserting it. That protective function is exactly why they cluster around unverified information.

  • He is believed to be dangerous.
  • The chief executive is understood to have left the country in March.
  • Two factories are expected to be closed before June.
  • There is said to be disagreement among the directors.

In institutional writing the passive keeps the affected item in subject position and avoids naming an individual: Your application has been received; the fee shall be paid within thirty days.

The ethical edge

The same structure that protects a newspaper can conceal an actor who ought to be named. Mistakes were made is grammatically impeccable and rhetorically evasive. Teach learners to notice the difference between an agent that is genuinely unknown and an agent that has been removed.

Information structure: theme, focus and prominence

English clauses tend to move from given information to new information. The first position — the theme — sets what the clause is about; the final position carries the focus. The passive is the main grammatical device for rearranging a clause so that the right element lands in each position.

Discourse needChooseExample

Put the new, newsworthy element lastPassive'Nice picture.' 'Yes, it was painted by my grandmother.'

When the active is the better sentence

  • When the agent is known, relevant and interesting — naming it is information, not clutter.
  • When a chain of passives has made the paragraph slow and abstract, and the reader has lost track of who is doing anything.
  • When the passive has been used to avoid responsibility that the text ought to assign.
  • When the active version is simply shorter and clearer. Brevity is a legitimate reason.

Reformulating a passive-heavy paragraph

Before

It is felt by the committee that the recommendations that were made in the previous report have not been implemented by the department, and it is therefore being suggested that a review should be conducted.
