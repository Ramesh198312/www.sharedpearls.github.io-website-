Is IELTS Academic right for you?

IELTS Academic measures whether your English is ready for an English-speaking academic or professional environment. Universities use it for undergraduate and postgraduate admission, and many professional bodies — for doctors, nurses, pharmacists, engineers and teachers, for example — use it for registration.

The test at a glance

Listening, Reading and Writing are taken in one sitting with no breaks between them. Speaking is a face-to-face conversation with a certified examiner, held on the same day or up to about a week before or after, depending on your test centre.

SectionTimeWhat you doQuestions
ListeningAbout 30 minutesFour recordings: an everyday conversation, an everyday monologue, an academic discussion and a university-style lecture. You hear each recording once.40
Reading60 minutesThree long texts from books, journals, magazines and newspapers, written for a non-specialist audience.40
Writing60 minutesTask 1: describe visual information in at least 150 words. Task 2: write an essay of at least 250 words in response to an argument or problem.2 tasks
Speaking11–14 minutesPart 1 interview, Part 2 long turn from a task card, Part 3 two-way discussion of more abstract ideas.3 parts

On paper, you get extra time at the end of Listening to copy your answers onto the answer sheet. On computer you type answers as you go and get a short review period instead. In Reading there is no extra transfer time in either format, so your answers must be on the answer sheet or screen within the 60 minutes.

How to book your test

  1. Confirm your requirementFind the exact overall and per-skill scores you need, the test version accepted and the latest date your results must be valid.
  2. Choose a format and a centreCompare computer and paper dates near you. Popular centres fill up quickly before university deadlines.
  3. Prepare your identificationYou must bring the same valid passport or national ID that you used when booking. A mismatch can stop you from sitting the test.
  4. Book and payFees are set locally and vary by country. Read the centre's rules on transfers, cancellations and refunds before paying.
  5. Request adjustments earlyIf you have a disability, a medical condition or a learning difference, ask the centre about modified papers, extra time or other arrangements well in advance.
  6. Plan your preparationUse the study plan builder to turn the weeks you have left into a realistic schedule.

On test day

Arrive early: identity checks, photographs and security procedures take time, and late arrivals may not be admitted. Personal belongings, including phones and smartwatches, are stored outside the test room. You will usually be given everything you need to write with.

Your results and what to do next

You receive a band score from 0 to 9 for each skill and an overall band. The overall band is the average of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band. Your results are shown on a Test Report Form (TRF).

Sending results

You can usually send results electronically to a number of organisations free of charge, and request more for a fee. Many organisations can also check results directly through a secure verification service.

How long results last

Most organisations accept results for two years from the test date. Some set a shorter limit, so check before you apply.

Asking for a remark

If you believe a score is wrong, you can request an Enquiry on Results, usually within six weeks of your test. You can ask for one or more skills to be re-marked. If your band changes, the fee is normally refunded.

IELTS One Skill Retake

If you took IELTS on computer at a participating centre, you may be able to retake one skill within 60 days instead of the whole test. Check first that your organisation accepts it.

Frequently asked questions

Is IELTS Academic harder than other versions?

Listening and Speaking are the same in every version. Academic Reading and Writing use academic-style texts and tasks, so they suit people heading for university or professional registration. The scoring standard is the same: a band 7 means the same level of ability whichever paper you take.

What do I write with in the paper test?

Listening and Reading answer sheets are completed in pencil. For Writing on paper you write on the answer sheet with the pen you are given. Always follow the instructions given in the room.

Does writing more words get a higher score?

No. Going over the minimum is not penalised, but length alone earns nothing. Writing too much in Task 1 can steal time from Task 2, which carries twice the weight.

Can I make notes during the test?

Yes. On paper you can write on the question paper; on computer you can use the highlighting and note tools. Notes are never marked — only your answers on the answer sheet or in the answer boxes count.

Is American or British spelling accepted?

Both are accepted, as long as you spell correctly. Try to stay consistent within one answer.

How many times can I take IELTS?

There is no limit on the number of attempts. Most improvement, however, comes from focused preparation between attempts rather than from repeating the test.

Will my Speaking test be recorded?

Yes. Speaking tests are recorded for quality control and so that they can be re-marked if you request an Enquiry on Results.

Why organisations rely on IELTS

IELTS Academic assesses all four skills — including a live Speaking test with a human examiner — and reports results on a single, well-described 0–9 scale. It is accepted by more than 13,500 organisations worldwide, which means applicants can reuse one result across several destinations.

What the band scores mean

Each band describes a level of practical ability. The labels below summarise the scale; the full skill-by-skill descriptions are published by the test owners.

BandLabelIn practice
9Expert userComplete, accurate and fluent command of the language.
8Very good userFull command with occasional slips; handles detailed argument well.
7Good userOperational command with some inaccuracies; generally handles complex language and reasoning.
6Competent userEffective command despite errors; copes with fairly complex language in familiar situations.
5Modest userPartial command; conveys overall meaning but makes many mistakes.
4Limited userBasic competence in familiar situations, with frequent problems of understanding and expression.
3–1Extremely limited to non-userVery restricted communication, down to isolated words.

The overall band is the average of the four skill scores, rounded to the nearest half band. Averages ending in .25 round up to the next half band, and averages ending in .75 round up to the next whole band.

Setting score requirements

A good requirement is defensible, specific and reviewed. A single overall score can hide a weak skill, so most organisations combine an overall minimum with per-skill minimums.

  1. Analyse the real language demandsList the tasks people must perform: following lectures, reading research, writing reports, speaking with patients or clients. Decide which skills matter most.
  2. Read the band descriptors with subject expertsAsk academic or professional staff which band best describes the minimum acceptable performance for each skill.
  3. Set overall and per-skill minimumsFor example, a writing-heavy programme might set a higher Writing minimum than a lab-based one; a patient-facing role might weight Speaking and Listening.
  4. Decide on validity and retakesState how old a result may be (two years is common) and whether you accept results improved through IELTS One Skill Retake or combined from several sittings.
  5. Monitor outcomesCompare entry scores with later academic or workplace performance and adjust if your thresholds prove too high or too low.

Verifying results

Never rely on a scanned or printed copy alone. Recognising organisations can register for the free IELTS Results Service and check scores directly against the official record.

  • Match the applicant's name, date of birth and photograph to the record.
  • Check the test date against your validity window.
  • Confirm the test version — Academic, General Training or a UKVI version — is the one you accept.
  • Record the TRF number and verification date in the applicant's file.
  • Treat any mismatch as a reason to pause the decision and contact the test owners, not as a minor clerical error.

New TRF design (from 15 July 2026): Test Report Forms now carry the logo and qualification title of Ofqual, the regulator for qualifications in England. Forms issued before and after that date remain valid, and test takers do not need to resend forms already submitted.

Security and integrity

Test security combines identity checks on test day, controlled test materials, examiner monitoring and statistical checks on results. For your own processes, the strongest protections are simple: verify every result at source, train staff to spot inconsistencies and record your checks.

In June 2026 the regulator Ofqual fined Cambridge English £875,000 after a marking fault in computer-delivered Listening and Reading tests between August 2023 and September 2025. Corrected results were issued, most of them higher by half a band. If you made decisions during that period, check whether any applicant received a corrected result. Understanding measurement error

No test measures ability perfectly. The standard error of measurement (SEM) estimates how far an observed score is likely to be from a candidate's "true" score. Roughly two times in three, the true score lies within one SEM of the observed score; about 95 times in a hundred, within two SEMs. This matters when a candidate falls just below a cut-off.

How validity is argued

Modern language testing treats validity as an argument built from many kinds of evidence, not as a single number. IELTS research is commonly organised around a socio-cognitive framework, which asks questions such as:

Test taker

Who takes the test, and are any groups disadvantaged by format, topic or delivery mode?

Cognitive validity

Do tasks trigger the same mental processes as real academic listening, reading, writing and speaking?

Context validity

Are the texts, tasks and conditions representative of the target situation?

Scoring validity

Are marking criteria, rater training and statistical checks consistent and fair?

Consequential validity

What effects do scores have on teaching (washback), on decisions and on society?

Criterion-related validity

How do scores relate to other measures and to later performance in study or work?

Insight: why Task 2 decides your Writing band

Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1. In practice, that means a candidate scoring 7 in Task 1 and 6 in Task 2 averages about 6.3, while the reverse — 6 in Task 1 and 7 in Task 2 — averages about 6.7. The same two numbers produce results almost half a band apart.

The lesson: give Task 2 the full 40 minutes, including five minutes of planning. A rushed, unfinished essay is the single most common reason strong candidates miss a Writing 7. Finish Task 1 in 20 minutes even if you would like to polish it.

Insight: what half a band really means

The published standard error of measurement for Academic Reading is 0.36 of a band, and for Listening 0.43. So if you need a 7 and score 6.5, your true ability may well be at 7 — but it may equally be at 6. Before rebooking, look at which question types you lost marks on. A targeted month of practice usually moves a score more reliably than an immediate retake.

Insight: computer-delivered tests, without the myths

  • Same test, same standard. The tasks, timing and marking criteria do not change with the format.
  • Different habits. On screen you cannot underline with a pen, so practise with highlighting tools and keep your eyes on the question numbers.
  • Faster results. Most computer results arrive within two days, which helps when an application deadline is close.
  • Retake option. Only computer-delivered tests can lead to IELTS One Skill Retake.

Insight: five habits of Band 8 readers

  1. They read the questions first and decide what kind of information each one needs — a name, a number, an opinion, a reason.
  2. They know that most question sets follow the order of the text, so they never search the whole passage for each answer.
  3. They treat paraphrase as the default: the passage almost never repeats the words of the question.
  4. They separate FALSE (the text says the opposite) from NOT GIVEN (the text does not say) and never answer from general knowledge.
  5. They move on after 90 seconds on one question and come back if time allows.

The six-stage preparation method

  1. Know the destinationWrite down the overall and per-skill bands you need and your deadline. Everything else is planned backwards from these numbers.
  2. Diagnose honestlyTake one complete timed test — Listening, Reading and Writing in one sitting, then a recorded Speaking test. Score it strictly. This is your baseline.
  3. Find the gapCompare your baseline with your target for each skill. A gap of half a band needs fine-tuning; a gap of a full band or more needs language development as well as technique.
  4. Train skills, not just testsDoing test after test only measures you. Improvement comes from focused practice: question types you miss, essay structures you avoid, sounds you mispronounce.
  5. Simulate under pressureEvery two or three weeks, sit a full test under exam timing with no pauses. Track your scores in a simple table.
  6. Taper and rehearseIn the final week, reduce the volume, revise your error log, rehearse the test-day routine and sleep properly.

Strategies for each section

Listening: anticipate, then follow

  • Use the reading time. Before each part, read the questions and predict the type of answer: a number, a date, a name, a noun.
  • Follow the signposts. Speakers mark changes with phrases such as "moving on to", "the main problem was", "actually, no — it's". Corrections are a favourite trap.
  • Spell perfectly. A correct idea with a spelling mistake scores zero. Learn the spelling of common topic words, days, months and numbers.
  • Watch the word limit. "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" is strict: three words is wrong even if the meaning is right.
  • Don't stop when you miss one. Let it go and listen for the next answer; answers come in order.

Practise with the 10 premium Listening tests →

Reading: navigate, don't read every word

  • Skim each passage in two minutes to learn its structure: what each paragraph is about.
  • Locate, then read closely. Find the part of the text that matches the question using key words and their paraphrases, then read those lines carefully.
  • Know the question logic. TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN tests facts; YES/NO/NOT GIVEN tests the writer's views. Headings test the main idea, not a detail.
  • Budget 20 minutes per passage — and remember there is no extra time to transfer answers.
  • Build reading stamina by reading one long article a day from a quality science, history or current-affairs source.

Practise with the 20 premium Reading tests →

Writing: plan, then write precisely

  • Task 1 in 20 minutes: introduction (paraphrase), overview of the main trends, two body paragraphs with selected data. No opinions.
  • Task 2 in 40 minutes: five minutes planning, a clear position, two or three well-developed body paragraphs, a conclusion that matches your introduction.
  • Answer every part of the question. Many essays lose marks because they discuss the topic instead of answering the question.
  • Keep an error log. Write down every correction you receive. Most candidates repeat the same five or six errors.

Open the full Writing resources →

Speaking: extend, explain, exemplify

  • Part 1: answer directly, then add a reason or an example. Two or three sentences is ideal.
  • Part 2: use the minute of preparation to note key words for each prompt, not full sentences. Keep talking until the examiner stops you.
  • Part 3: give opinions, compare, speculate about the future and consider other viewpoints.
  • Record yourself weekly and listen for hesitations, repeated words and unclear sounds.

Preparation mistakes to avoid

Testing instead of training

Doing a full test every day measures your level but rarely changes it. Spend most of your time on targeted practice.

Memorising model essays

Examiners recognise memorised material, and it rarely fits the actual question. Learn structures and flexible language instead.

Ignoring timing

Practising without a clock hides your real performance. Time every task from week two.

Chasing rare vocabulary

Precise, natural word choice scores higher than impressive words used wrongly.

Reading sample: a short passage

Allow about 15 minutes. A full Reading passage is longer — around 700–900 words — but the question types are the same.

The Return of the Night Train

ASleeper trains were once a familiar part of European travel. In the 1970s, an extensive network of overnight services linked cities from Lisbon to Moscow, carrying business travellers, holidaymakers and families who preferred a bed to a long drive. Over the following decades, however, the network contracted sharply. Cheap flights, faster daytime trains and the high cost of maintaining specialised carriages made night services unprofitable, and many operators withdrew them altogether.

BThe decline was not universal. Austria's national railway chose to expand rather than retreat, taking over several routes abandoned by other operators in the mid-2010s and ordering a new generation of carriages. Its gamble rested on a simple observation: a night train replaces not only a journey but also a night in a hotel, and on trips of roughly 800 to 1,500 kilometres it can compete with flying on total travel time once airport transfers and security checks are included.

CInterest has since grown, driven partly by travellers' concern about the climate. A passenger travelling by electric train typically produces a small fraction of the emissions of the same journey by air, although the exact figure depends on how the electricity is generated. Surveys suggest that many travellers say they would choose a sleeper for environmental reasons, but researchers caution that stated intentions do not always translate into bookings, particularly when fares are high.

DPrice remains the central difficulty. A sleeper carriage carries far fewer passengers than a day carriage of the same length, because much of its space is given over to beds, and each train completes only one journey in twenty-four hours. Track access charges, which operators pay to use the rail network, vary widely between countries and can add significantly to the cost of a long international route. Some governments now subsidise night services or reduce these charges, arguing that the environmental benefits justify public support.

EThe future of the night train may depend less on nostalgia than on design. New carriages offer private compartments with their own washrooms, aimed at travellers who once rejected sleepers as cramped and uncomfortable, alongside cheaper reclining seats for those on a budget. Whether such improvements can attract enough passengers to make routes viable without permanent subsidy is a question operators are still trying to answer.

Writing sample: Task 1

You should spend about 20 minutes on this task. The chart below shows the average number of hours per week spent on four leisure activities by two age groups in one country in 2025. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. Write at least 150 words.

Read a high-band model answer (184 words)

The bar chart compares how many hours a week people aged 16–24 and those aged 65 and over spent on four leisure activities in one country in 2025.

Overall, the two age groups had strikingly different preferences. Older people devoted by far the most time to television, whereas the younger group favoured online video. Sport and exercise occupied relatively little time in both groups.

The most dramatic contrast concerned screen-based entertainment. People aged 65 and over watched television for an average of 20 hours a week, more than three times the figure for 16- to 24-year-olds (6 hours). The pattern was almost exactly reversed for online video: the younger group spent 14 hours a week on it, compared with only 3 hours among older people.

Reading was also more popular with the older group, who read for 6 hours a week, three times as long as the young (2 hours). Sport and exercise was the second activity in which the younger group led, though by a much smaller margin: 5 hours compared with 3. For both groups, however, it remained a relatively minor pastime.

Why this response scores highly

  • Task achievement: a clear overview in the second paragraph; key figures are selected and compared rather than listed one by one.
  • Coherence and cohesion: paragraphs group related activities; linking is natural ("whereas", "The pattern was almost exactly reversed").
  • Lexical resource: precise comparison language ("more than three times the figure", "devoted by far the most time").
  • Grammar: a range of structures — comparatives, relative clauses, participle phrases — with no errors.

Writing sample: Task 2

You should spend about 40 minutes on this task. Some people believe that universities should focus mainly on preparing students for employment. Others think that their main purpose is to provide knowledge for its own sake. Discuss both views and give your own opinion. Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience. Write at least 250 words.

Read a high-band model answer (298 words)

Universities have always served more than one master. While some argue that their chief responsibility is to equip graduates for the job market, others maintain that the pursuit of knowledge is valuable in itself. In my view, the two aims are less opposed than they first appear, but a university that serves only employers would fail both students and society.

The case for an employment focus is understandable. Higher education is expensive, and many students borrow heavily to pay for it. They reasonably expect their degree to lead to secure work, and governments that fund universities want a return in the form of a skilled workforce. Courses in medicine, engineering or accounting, which combine theory with professional training, show how effectively universities can prepare people for specific careers.

On the other hand, knowledge pursued for its own sake has produced many of the discoveries that later transformed economies. Research into the properties of light or the structure of DNA was not designed to create jobs, yet it eventually underpinned entire industries. Moreover, subjects such as history and philosophy develop the ability to analyse evidence and construct arguments — qualities employers increasingly value precisely because they cannot be taught as narrow job skills.

For these reasons, I believe universities should prioritise deep, rigorous learning while making its practical value visible. Work placements, career guidance and projects with local organisations can be added to almost any degree without reducing it to vocational training. What matters is that the curriculum is not dictated by the needs of the labour market in a particular year, which may be very different by the time students graduate.

In conclusion, preparing students for work and expanding knowledge are complementary goals, but the second should lead, because it is the foundation on which lasting employability is built.

Why this response scores highly

  • Task response: both views are discussed fairly, and the writer's position is stated in the introduction, developed in paragraph 4 and confirmed in the conclusion.
  • Coherence and cohesion: one central idea per paragraph; reference ("these reasons", "the second") avoids repetition.
  • Lexical resource: natural collocations such as "serve more than one master", "underpinned entire industries", "dictated by the needs of the labour market".
  • Grammar: complex sentences are controlled and accurate, including concessive and relative clauses.

Task 1: every type of visual

Task 1 asks you to summarise, select and compare. You never give opinions or reasons the data does not show.

📈

Line graph

Focus on trends over time: the overall direction, peaks, troughs and crossover points.

📊

Bar chart

Compare categories. Group bars that behave similarly instead of describing each one.

🥧

Pie chart

Proportions of a whole. Use fractions and percentages; compare the largest and smallest shares.

🔢

Table

Lots of numbers — select. Find the highest, lowest and most surprising figures.

⚙️

Describe stages in order using the passive and sequencing language. Count the stages in your overview.

🗺️

Map

Describe changes between two or more dates: what was added, removed, expanded or relocated.

The four-paragraph structure

  1. Introduction (1 sentence)Paraphrase what the visual shows: what, where, when, and in what units.
  2. Overview (2 sentences)The two or three most important features — the big picture, with no detailed numbers. This is the single most important paragraph for Task Achievement.
  3. Body paragraph 1Details of the first group of features, with selected figures and comparisons.
  4. Body paragraph 2Details of the second group of features. Stop at around 170–190 words.

Task 2: the five essay types

Identify the type in the first ten seconds — it decides your structure and your thesis.

Opinion (agree or disagree / to what extent)

Recognise it: "To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

Structure: Introduction with a clear position → two body paragraphs supporting it (optionally one conceding the other side) → conclusion restating your position.

Thesis model: "While X has some advantages, I strongly disagree that it should replace Y, because…"

Discussion (discuss both views and give your opinion)

Recognise it: "Some people think…; others believe… Discuss both views and give your own opinion."

Structure: Introduction outlining both views and your opinion → body 1 view A → body 2 view B (or your view) → conclusion.

Trap: forgetting your own opinion, which caps Task Response.

Advantages and disadvantages

Recognise it: "Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" or "What are the advantages and disadvantages?"

Structure: If asked whether one outweighs the other, you must give a judgement and keep it consistent throughout.

Problem and solution (causes and solutions)

Recognise it: "What problems does this cause? What can be done to solve them?"

Structure: body 1 problems/causes, explained with consequences → body 2 solutions matched to those problems.

Tip: link each solution explicitly to a problem you described.

Two-part (direct) questions

Recognise it: two separate questions, e.g. "Why is this happening? Is it a positive or negative development?"

Structure: one body paragraph per question, answered directly in its first sentence.

Building a Band 7+ body paragraph

Point Public transport investment is the most effective way to reduce urban congestion. Explain Unlike road building, which tends to attract more cars, efficient trains and buses give drivers a realistic alternative. Example In cities with frequent and reliable metro services, a far smaller share of commuters choose to drive into the centre. Link Spending on public transport therefore tackles the cause of congestion rather than its symptoms.

Overall band calculator

Planning for a target profile

Many universities and regulators set a minimum for each skill as well as for the overall band. Use the overall calculator to test different profiles: for example, 7.5, 7.5, 6.0 and 6.5 average 6.875, which rounds to an overall 7.0 — but if your course requires 6.5 in every skill, the 6.0 in Writing still means you have not met the requirement.

Strategy: work out which skill is cheapest to improve. For many candidates, Listening and Reading respond fastest to targeted practice, while Writing and Speaking need longer, feedback-driven work.

The three parts

PartLengthWhat happensAim for
1 · Interview4–5 minutesQuestions about familiar topics: home, studies, work, hobbies, daily routines.Direct answer + reason or example (2–3 sentences).
2 · Long turn3–4 minutesA task card with a topic and prompts. One minute to prepare, then speak for up to two minutes. One or two short follow-up questions.A continuous, organised talk that covers every prompt.
3 · Discussion4–5 minutesAbstract questions linked to the Part 2 topic: society, change, comparison, the future.Opinions developed with reasons, examples and balance.

The whole test lasts 11–14 minutes and is recorded. It is a real conversation with a certified examiner, in person or by video call depending on your centre.

What examiners listen for

Fluency & Coherence

Speaking at length without unnatural pauses, and linking ideas so that the listener can follow easily.

Lexical Resource

Vocabulary that is precise and natural, including idiomatic language used appropriately and the ability to paraphrase.

Grammatical Range & Accuracy

A mix of simple and complex structures, with errors that don't get in the way of meaning.

Pronunciation

Clear individual sounds, word and sentence stress, rhythm and intonation. An accent is fine; unclear speech is not.

Strategies for each part

  • Answer the question first, then extend: "Yes, quite a lot, actually — mainly because…"
  • Use tenses naturally: past for change ("I used to…"), present perfect for experience ("I've recently started…").
  • Avoid one-word answers and memorised speeches; both sound unnatural.
  • Note four or five key words — one per prompt — plus one story or detail you can describe vividly.
  • Follow the prompts in order; they give your talk a ready-made structure.
  • Spend most of your time on the final "explain why" prompt: it invites the richest language.
  • If you finish early, add a comparison, a feeling or a consequence.
  • Use the O-R-E pattern: Opinion → Reason → Example (or Exception).
  • Speculate: "It's quite likely that…", "I'd imagine that in the future…"
  • Compare generations, countries or past and present.
  • It's fine to say you haven't thought about something before — then think aloud in good English.

Language that lifts your band

Buying thinking time

  • That's an interesting question — I haven't really thought about it before.
  • Let me think… I suppose the main reason would be…

Giving opinions

  • I'd argue that… / I'm fairly convinced that…
  • To some extent, I'd say… but…

Comparing

  • Compared with my parents' generation, …
  • Whereas in the past…, these days…

Paraphrasing a word you've forgotten

  • It's a kind of… / It's the thing you use to…
  • I can't think of the exact word, but it's similar to…

Pronunciation quick wins

  1. Word stress: PHOtograph, phoTOgrapher, photoGRAPHic — stress shifts with the suffix.
  2. Sentence stress: stress the content words — I SPENT the WHOLE SUMmer LEARNing to SWIM.
  3. Chunking: pause between meaningful groups of words, not in the middle of them.
  4. Final consonants: don't drop the ends of words — asked, months, world.
  5. Intonation: fall for statements, rise to show you're adding more or checking.

Test day: what to expect

A calm, well-rehearsed test day is worth marks. Here is what happens, the rules you must follow, and what to do after the test.

← Back to the Premium dashboard

The day before

  • Check your confirmation email for the reporting time, address and any centre-specific instructions.
  • Put your identification document by the door. It must be the same document you used to book, and it must be valid.
  • Plan your journey with a margin for delays; late candidates may not be allowed in.
  • Do light revision only: skim your error log and a few useful phrases. No full mock test.
  • Sleep. Concentration in the third hour of the test depends on it.

How the day unfolds

  1. Check-inIdentity verification, photograph and, at many centres, a finger scan. Belongings go into a secure storage area.
  2. Listening (about 30 minutes)Instructions and recordings play through headphones or speakers. Read ahead whenever you are given time.
  3. Reading (60 minutes)Three passages, 40 questions. Keep to about 20 minutes per passage — no extra transfer time.
  4. Writing (60 minutes)Task 1, then Task 2 — or Task 2 first if that is your strategy. Watch the clock, not the word count.
  5. Speaking (11–14 minutes)Same day or a different day, depending on your centre. The examiner checks your ID again before starting.

Rules you must follow

Allowed in the room

  • Your ID document
  • Pencils, pen and eraser (paper test; often provided)
  • A clear water bottle, where the centre permits it

Not allowed

  • Phones, smartwatches and any electronic device
  • Notes, dictionaries or paper of your own
  • Talking to or copying from other candidates

Breaking the rules can lead to your results being withheld or cancelled. If you feel unwell or have a problem with the recording or equipment, raise your hand and tell the invigilator immediately — not after the test.
