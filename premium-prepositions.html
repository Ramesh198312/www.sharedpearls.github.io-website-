01 — Reading the Compass

What a preposition actually does

Prepositions show relationships in time and space, and relationships between ideas — logical relationships. A preposition in a prepositional phrase is always followed by a noun, a pronoun, or another word standing in for one, such as an -ing form.

Always use the object form of a personal pronoun after a preposition — never the subject form.

Robert went with Carol. → Robert went with her.
never "with she"

Many words that work as prepositions can also work as adverbs — but not every one of them can, and the two roles are easy to mix up.

Preposition or adverb?
Shirley is waiting outside the door. (prepositional phrase, used adverbially)
Shirley is waiting outside. (adverb, alone)

Some adverbs need a preposition added before they can take an object at all: away and far are adverbs on their own, but away from and far from are the prepositional forms.

Watch out for these problems

Confusing "in front of" with "opposite"
The park is in front of the cinema. (meaning: facing it, across the road)
The park is opposite the cinema.
Dropping part of the preposition
It is next the supermarket. / The post box is in front the post office. / She stood on top the hill.
next to · in front of · on top of — none of these can lose a word.

Place and movement, side by side

Some prepositions describe either place or movement — over, above, in, outside. Others pair closely: a movement lands you in a related place.

Watch out for these problems

The wrong preposition of movement
Gabi walked between the forest.
Gabi walked through the forest.
Movement used where place is needed
I'll meet you into the kitchen. · I arrived into the station. · The children were sitting towards the teacher. · There was a beautiful painting through the corridor.
meet / arrive → in, at  ·  a static position never takes towards, into, or through.

03 — Beyond Space and Time

Logical prepositions and set collocations

Not every preposition marks a place or a moment. Many mark a logical relationship — part to whole, origin, cause, or a fixed partnership with a particular adjective, noun, or verb.

Part and whole: partitive "of"

When one comes before of, one is the subject of the clause and takes a singular verb — even though the noun after of is plural.

ExampleNote
One of our friends has a car.singular verb, plural noun after "of"
Many of them have cars.plural nouns take plural pronouns
Much of the water is polluted.uncountable noun — singular verb
Some of the rice has been burned.uncountable noun, can be replaced by "it"
None of the information was helpful.uncountable, singular verb

Origin and material: of, from, out of

ExampleMeaning
Madan Krishna is a citizen of Nepal.nationality / citizenship
Sara is a student from Mexico.her home is in Mexico
George is a student of Mexico.he studies about Mexico
Amanda is a doctor from Kathmandu.her home is in Kathmandu
Amanda is a doctor of medicine.her field / kind of doctor
The desk is made of / from / out of wood.material

06 — For Teachers

Three ready-to-teach, 60-minute lessons

Each lesson is built entirely around the categories, examples, and common errors already mapped on this page, so what students practise here in class matches exactly what they'll meet above. Tap a lesson to open the full plan, or download it as an editable document.

Lesson 1

Mapping the Room

Prepositions of Place · 60 minutes
⌄
Learning objectives
  • Accurately produce at least 12 prepositions of place in spoken and written descriptions.
  • Distinguish commonly confused pairs: in front of vs opposite; on vs on top of.
  • Describe the position of objects in a real or pictured space with at least 80% accuracy in a paired task.
Target prepositions

on · in / inside / at · on top of · above / over · beneath / underneath / below · in front of · behind · opposite · outside · near / close by · beside / next to · between / in between · beyond · in the middle of

Learning outcomes
  • Can describe a room layout to a partner who cannot see it.
  • Can give and receive a description and correctly place objects from it.
  • Can self-correct the two error patterns flagged in class.
Prior knowledge required

Basic sentence structure with "be" (There is / are…); classroom and furniture vocabulary; enough confidence to describe simple objects aloud.

Student-centred approach

Every new preposition is elicited from students before the teacher confirms it — nothing is presented as a rule first. Students then move immediately into pair work (guided discovery → collaborative information-gap → communicative mingle), with the teacher acting as monitor and error-collector rather than lecturer for roughly 45 of the 60 minutes.

60-minute timeline

0–5 min

Warm-up: "Where's my pen?"

Teacher prepares: a small object (a pen) and a box or book.

Teacher does: silently places the pen in a series of positions relative to the box (on it, under it, behind it…) and mimes "where is it?"

Students do: call out or point to a guess, in English if they can, in L1 if they can't yet — no correction at this stage, purely noticing.

5–15 min

Guided discovery

Teacher does: repeats the object-movement 8–10 times, now asking pairs to write their guess on a mini-whiteboard before confirming. Builds a live board reference grouped by meaning family (touching a surface: on, on top of; enclosed: in, inside; higher, not touching: above, over…), mirroring the categories on this page.

Monitoring: teacher watches whiteboards for the two watch-out errors before they're even named, so the next stage lands with real classroom examples.

15–23 min

Explicit clarification

Teacher does: presents the two confusable pairs directly from this page's "Watch out" box — in front of vs opposite, and the words that can never lose "of" or "to" (on top of, next to). Runs 3–4 concept-check questions: "If two houses face each other across a road, are they in front of each other or opposite each other?"

Transition: "Now let's put this on paper" → controlled practice.

23–31 min

Controlled practice

Students do: complete Worksheet 1, Section B (10-item gap-fill) individually, then compare answers in pairs before the teacher gives the answer key.

Teacher does: circulates, notes recurring mistakes for the feedback stage.

31–43 min

Collaborative activity: "Describe the Room" information gap

Teacher prepares: Worksheet 1's two room grids (Version A and Version B), a class set, plus a book each pair can stand upright as a divider.

Students do: in A/B pairs, without showing their own grid, Student A describes where eight objects are; Student B fills in their blank grid from the description. Swap roles with the second grid. Pairs compare grids at the end to check accuracy.

Teacher monitors: listens for natural use of the target prepositions and intervenes only for breakdowns in communication, not minor errors.

43–53 min

Communicative application: "My Dream Room"

Students do: sketch a simple stick-figure floor plan of an ideal study space (30 seconds is enough), then describe it to a partner using at least six target prepositions. The partner asks one clarifying question using a preposition ("Is the desk beside the window or opposite it?"). Rotate once to a new partner.

Teacher does: collects 3–4 real learner sentences (anonymised) while circulating, for the next stage.

53–57 min

Feedback and error correction

Teacher does: writes the collected sentences on the board without names attached; the class corrects them together, tying each fix back to the two watch-out patterns taught earlier.

57–60 min

Assessment and reflection: exit ticket

Students do: write one sentence describing where their bag or phone is right now, using a preposition not yet said aloud in class today, and hand it in on the way out.

Homework: describe your bedroom in five sentences, using five different prepositions of place, and underline each one.

Materials and resources

ResourceHow it's used
Small object + boxSilent demonstration for the warm-up and guided discovery.
Mini-whiteboards or scrap paperPairs write guesses before whole-class confirmation.

Differentiation and support

Additional support: provide a sentence frame card ("The ___ is ___ the ___.") for the information-gap task; pre-teach the eight object nouns before pairs begin; accept single-word or pointing answers during the warm-up.
Greater challenge: extension cards asking students to combine two prepositions in one sentence ("The lamp is on the desk, between the books and the window"), or to write instructions for a robot rearranging furniture.

Assessment and reflection

Formative checks run throughout: whiteboard guesses in guided discovery, peer-checked gap-fill, teacher monitoring during the information gap and mingle, and the written exit ticket as a final individual check against the lesson objective. Recurring errors feed directly into the whole-class feedback stage rather than being corrected in isolation.

Lesson 2

Finding the Way

Prepositions of Movement · 60 minutes
⌄
Learning objectives
  • Give and follow spoken directions using at least eight prepositions of movement.
  • Correctly distinguish movement from place prepositions in context (e.g. never "arrive into").
  • Complete a map route description with at least 80% accuracy in a paired task.
Target prepositions

to / towards / up to · from / away from · into / out of · onto / off · round / around · over · through · along · past

Learning outcomes
  • Can give a multi-step direction a partner can actually follow.
  • Can correctly match a movement preposition to the place it results in.
  • Can identify and fix the two error patterns flagged in class.
Prior knowledge required

The imperative form (turn, go, walk); basic direction vocabulary (left, right, straight on). Lesson 1's place prepositions are helpful but not required.

Student-centred approach

The lesson borrows this page's own storytelling method — narrating a journey and pausing before each preposition for students to supply it — before handing control to students for two problem-solving tasks: a map-based information gap and a "human robot" navigation game, both requiring precise, negotiated language rather than teacher-supplied answers.

60-minute timeline

0–5 min

Warm-up: physical Simon Says

Teacher does: gives simple physical instructions using target prepositions ("walk around your chair," "step over the bag," "reach under the desk"). Students act them out at their desks — kinesthetic noticing, no correction yet.

5–13 min

Guided discovery: the journey story

Teacher does: narrates a short journey (a character walking to work or school) while moving a token along a route sketched on the board, pausing before each preposition for students to supply it before confirming and adding it to a labelled route diagram.

13–21 min

Explicit clarification

Teacher does: walks through this page's place–movement correspondence table (climb onto the roof → be on top of it) and the two flagged errors — a movement preposition used for a static position ("I'll meet you into the kitchen") and the wrong preposition of movement ("walked between the forest"). CCQ: "Do we ever arrive into somewhere?"

21–28 min

Controlled practice

Students do: complete Worksheet 2, Section B (10-item gap-fill following a short character's journey) individually, then pair-check before the answer key.

28–43 min

Collaborative map-based problem-solving

Teacher prepares: Worksheet 2's town map handouts — Student A and Student B versions, each with a different secret destination marked from the same starting point.

Students do: Student A gives step-by-step directions to their secret destination; Student B traces the route on their own copy and names where they end up. Swap and repeat with a new destination.

Teacher monitors: checks whether the named destination actually matches — a direct, self-correcting accuracy check.

43–53 min

Communicative application: "Teacher Robot"

Students do: in groups of three, one student is the "robot" (facing away or eyes closed); the other two give precise step-by-step instructions using movement prepositions to guide the robot to a target object without touching anything else. Rotate the robot role.

53–57 min

Feedback and error correction

Teacher does: shares 3–4 real (anonymised) sentences overheard while monitoring; the class corrects them together, tying each fix back to the two watch-out patterns.

57–60 min

Assessment and reflection: exit ticket

Students do: write one sentence giving a direction from the classroom door to their own seat, using at least one movement preposition.

Homework: write directions from home to the nearest shop, using at least six prepositions of movement.

Materials and resources

ResourceHow it's used
Small token/figureTraces the route during the board narration.
Worksheet 2, Section BControlled gap-fill on a character's journey.
Worksheet 2 town-map handouts (A & B)The map-based information-gap task.

Differentiation and support

Additional support: a word bank plus sentence frame ("Go ___ the ___.") for the map task; allow single-preposition instructions with pointing in "Teacher Robot."
Greater challenge: require a minimum six-step, six-preposition route with no repeats; ask fast finishers to re-describe the same route using different but equally correct prepositions (over vs above).

Assessment and reflection

The map task is self-marking (the wrong destination reveals a language breakdown immediately), which frees the teacher to monitor language rather than manage the task. Combined with the gap-fill, "Teacher Robot," and exit ticket, the lesson checks the objective from four independent angles.

Lesson 3

When Does It Happen?

Prepositions of Time · 60 minutes
⌄
Learning objectives
  • Correctly select on / at / in for at least 90% of given time expressions.
  • Use for and since accurately in present-perfect sentences.
  • Describe a personal weekly schedule using at least eight prepositions of time.
Target prepositions

on · at · in · by · until / till · through (AmE) · after · before · during · for · since

Learning outcomes
  • Can build a timetable through spoken questions and answers.
  • Can describe their own week using a spread of time prepositions.
  • Can explain, in their own words, the difference between for and since.
Prior knowledge required

Days, months, and clock-time vocabulary; basic present simple; light exposure to the present perfect is helpful for for/since but not essential.

Student-centred approach

Students build the on/at/in rule themselves from a real timetable before it is ever named as a rule, then immediately use it to complete a genuine information gap (an exchange student's timetable) and to talk about their own lives — the two most reliable ways to make a time-preposition choice automatic rather than memorised.

60-minute timeline

0–5 min

Warm-up: call-out quiz

Teacher does: reads time expressions aloud (Saturday… the weekend… summer… three o'clock); students call out the matching preposition as a class, or hold up pre-made in/on/at cards.

5–13 min

Guided discovery: the real timetable

Teacher does: projects or shows a personal weekly diary and thinks aloud, building example sentences ("I go swimming ___ Monday… I eat lunch ___ midday…"), eliciting the missing preposition each time. Students complete a live on/at/in rule chart on the board as it emerges (days & dates / clock times, day-parts & the weekend / years, months, seasons).

13–21 min

Explicit clarification

Teacher does: contrasts the trickier pairs from this page — by vs until (before an end time vs up to it); during vs for (within a period vs its length); for vs since (length vs starting point, with the present perfect). CCQ, using the page's own example: "Ariel's worked in London since October — did she work there in September?"

21–28 min

Controlled practice

Students do: complete Worksheet 3, Section B (10-item gap-fill in personal-schedule contexts) individually, then pair-check before the answer key.

28–43 min

Collaborative timetable information gap

Teacher prepares: Worksheet 3's two half-timetables for a fictional exchange student's week — Student A has Monday–Wednesday, Student B has Thursday–Saturday, both with gaps in the other days.

Students do: ask and answer questions ("When does she have Art class?" — "On Thursday, at ten o'clock") to complete both timetables, then jointly write three sentences summarising the week using during, for, and since.

43–53 min

Communicative application: "My Real Week"

Students do: complete a short personal weekly-schedule grid (5 minutes), then describe their own week to a new partner ("I have English on Tuesdays and Thursdays, at nine o'clock. I've been learning English since [year] / for [number] years."). The partner asks one follow-up question using a time preposition.

53–57 min

Feedback and error correction

Teacher does: shares anonymised errors collected while monitoring, focused on the for/since and by/until confusions, and corrects them as a class.

57–60 min

Assessment and reflection: exit ticket

Students do: write one true sentence about themselves using since, and one using for, and hand both in.

Homework: write five sentences about your week, each using a different one of on, at, in, for, and since.

Materials and resources

ResourceHow it's used
A personal weekly timetable (shown or printed)Source material for guided discovery.
In/On/At cards or mini-whiteboardsFast whole-class response in the warm-up.
Worksheet 3, Section BControlled gap-fill practice.
Worksheet 3 timetable handouts (A & B)The exchange-student information gap.

Differentiation and support

Additional support: sentence frame ("I have ___ class ___ [day], at ___.") and a visible on/at/in reference chart kept up throughout; accept single-clause answers in the timetable gap.
Greater challenge: require during, for, and since all to appear correctly in "My Real Week"; extension card asking students to explain in their own words why since needs a present-perfect verb.

Assessment and reflection

The call-out quiz gives an instant whole-class read on on/at/in before anything is explained; the timetable gap is self-checking (a wrong preposition produces a wrong answer the partner can query); and the exit ticket isolates the for/since distinction specifically, since it's the pairing students find hardest to automate.

  • In/On/At cards or mini-whiteboards
  • Worksheet 3 printed — class set
  • Timetable handouts A & B cut/copied separately
  • Blank weekly-schedule grid
  • Exit-ticket slips
  • Worksheet 3 answer key on hand
