Conditionals:
If, When, Unless — and Beyond

All four conditional types explained with professional examples, business scenarios, and interactive exercises — including the tricky mixed conditionals.

What Are Conditionals?

Conditionals describe situations where one event depends on another. They are essential in professional English — for proposals, negotiations, planning, and polite requests. English has four main conditional types, each expressing a different degree of reality or possibility.

The Four Conditionals — Interactive Guide

Conditional 0
The Zero Conditional
For universal truths, scientific facts, habits, and standard organizational procedures.

If/When + Present Simple, → Present Simple

Scientific Fact

If you heat water to 100°C, it boils.

Corporate SLA

If a client raises a high-priority ticket, our support team responds within 30 minutes.

Economic Rule

When market demand increases significantly, product prices usually rise.

General Habit

If I work late into the evening, I drink black tea to stay focused.

Company Policy

When employees travel on business, the firm reimburses all verified lodging expenses.

Conditional 1
The First Conditional
For real, highly likely future situations — operational plans, warnings, and contractual promises.

If + Present Simple, → will / can / may + Infinitive

Everyday Logistics

If it rains heavily tomorrow morning, I will take the expressway.

Strategic Planning

If we secure the enterprise contract, we will recruit five senior developers.

Sales Negotiation

If your organization places the order today, we will waive all shipping fees.

Operational Risk

If the supplier delays the shipment again, we may terminate the contract.

Personal Promise

If you finish drafting the summary by 4 PM, I will review it immediately.

Conditional 2
The Second Conditional
For hypothetical, imaginary, or unlikely present/future events, and for high-level diplomatic diplomacy.

If + Past Simple, → would / could / might + Infinitive

Hypothetical Scenario

If I had a higher travel allowance, I would attend every international summit.

Market Expansion

If we expanded into Southeast Asia, we would require localized marketing strategies.

Polite Business Request

I would appreciate it immensely if you could send the audit files by Friday.

Corporate Restructuring

If our founder stepped down, the board might appoint an external interim CEO.

Personal Reflection

If I were in your position, I would negotiate for flexible remote working hours.

Conditional 3
The Third Conditional
For unchangeable past events — analyzing past mistakes, expressing regret, or assessing missed opportunities.

If + Past Perfect, → would / could / might have + Past Participle

Past Regret

If she had prepared more thoroughly, she would have passed the board interview.

Business Post-Mortem

If we had launched the feature in Q1, we would have captured a majority market share.

Customer Relations

If the account manager had notified us sooner, we could have resolved the glitch immediately.

Financial Audit

If the team had double-checked the ledger, they would not have made such an error.

Personal Opportunity

If I had known you were visiting London, I would have met you for lunch.

Mixed Conditionals

Mixed conditionals bridge time frames by linking a past condition to a present result, or a permanent present state to a past outcome. They are indispensable for advanced analytical communication.

Pattern 1: Past Action → Present Consequence

Structure: If + Past Perfect (Had + V3), → Would + Base Verb

Use: Connects a past action or decision to a current ongoing state or present reality.

• "If we had invested in AI infrastructure five years ago, our company would be the market leader today."

• "If she had completed her doctorate last year, she would hold a tenured faculty post now."

• "If I had accepted that job transfer to Geneva, I would live near the mountains right now."

Pattern 2: Present State → Past Outcome

Structure: If + Past Simple, → Would have + Past Participle (V3)

Use: Explains how a permanent character trait, continuous skill, or ongoing present reality directly altered a past event.

• "If he were a more meticulous project leader, he would have noticed the budget deficit earlier."

• "If our software platform were more stable, yesterday's migration would not have failed."

• "If I spoke fluent Spanish, I would have applied for the South American regional manager role."

Unless, Provided That & As Long As

Beyond "if", professionals regularly use these conditional connectors — and using them correctly signals a strong command of English.

WordMeaningProfessional Example
Unless= if not"Unless you confirm by Friday, we will proceed without your input."
Provided that= only if (condition)"We will sign the contract, provided that all terms are agreed."
As long as= on the condition that"We can offer flexible hours as long as targets are met."
In case= to prepare for a possibility"Please keep a copy of all correspondence in case of a dispute."
Supposing= what if (hypothetical)"Supposing the deal falls through — what is our contingency plan?"

Identify the Conditional Type & Structure
Analyze the grammatical structure and temporal logic of each sentence below
1. "If we lose this key account, we will need to restructure our revenue forecast immediately."
2. "If I had known about the severe budget cuts, I would have planned our marketing campaign differently."
3. "When a client submits an official complaint, our quality assurance team investigates the case within 24 hours."
4. "If we had merged with that logistics firm two years ago, we would dominate the regional supply market today."
5. "If I were the Chief Executive Officer, I would completely restructure the international sales division."
6. "If the lead architect were more detail-oriented, he would not have overlooked the structural flaws in last week's design."

8. "If the research team had identified the flaw during clinical trials, the drug would not have been recalled."

12. "We could acquire our primary competitor if we secured additional venture capital financing next quarter."

Professional Tip

Second Conditional = Polite Requests

In professional English, the Second Conditional softens directives into diplomatic inquiries:

✗ "Can you send me the file?"
✓ "I would appreciate it if you could send me the file."

Common Pitfalls

  • "If I would go" ✗ (Use "If I went")
  • "If I would have" ✗ (Use "If I had")
  • "Unless you don't" ✗ (Double negative)
  • In case vs. If in contracts
